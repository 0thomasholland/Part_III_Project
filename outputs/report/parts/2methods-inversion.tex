\subsection{Bayesian Inversion}

% Breif introduction

By applying the inversion framework outlined in \autoref{sec:bay}, prior knowledge of SL physics is encoded in prior distributions reflecting the state of the system, yielding an updated posterior belief of the state of the model space based on observations made in the data space.



\subsubsection{Simple inversion}

\notebookcite{thomasholland06SimpleInversionipynb2026}

A simple case for this is to invert for ice thickness change, $\thicknesschangeice$, from SSH altimetry data, $d$, where the forward problem takes the form

\begin{align}
	d &= \operatoraltimetry{\text{ocean}} \circ \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadice \left(\thicknesschangeice\right) + n,\\
	d &= \operator{O}{simple}\left(\thicknesschangeice\right) + n, \label{eq:simple_case}
\end{align}

where $\operator{O}{simple}$ is the composed forward operator for this simple case and $n$ represents the altimetry noise. The noise is taken to be an independent Gaussian measure in the dataspace with zero mean and covariance $\covariance{n}$:

\begin{equation}
	n \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \covariance{n}\right).
\end{equation}

A true ice-thickness field, $\thicknesschangeice^\dagger$,  is drawn as a single sample from the prior measure $\thicknesschangeice$, providing a plausible but non-trivial test case that is, by construction, consistent with our prior assumptions. Synthetic altimetry observations, $\dobs$, are then generated by passing through the forward operator and adding the Gaussian noise:

\begin{equation}
	\dobs = \operator{O}{simple}\left(\thicknesschangeice^\dagger\right) + n
\end{equation}

Using the Bayesian inversion framework, we generate the posterior model space measure, $\thicknesschangeice^{p}$, which can be inspected to understand the new inversion method. The posterior covariance between major sources is also inspected to assess how well the method disambiguates between them.

\subsubsection{Comparison with previous method}
\notebookcite{thomasholland06SimpleInversionipynb2026}

For comparison with the previous method of estimating GMSLC from SSHC the $z$-scores of both methods relative to the true GMSLC are compared to assess their performance in terms of accuracy and uncertainty quantification, as the $z$-score is a standardised measure of how many standard deviations the true GMSLC is from the mean estimate of each method.

We generate a true GMSL value, $\gmslctrue$, and the inversion method's estimate, $\gmslcbay$ by applying the mass balance GMSL operator, $\operatorgmsl{\text{true}}$, to the true ice thickness change field and the posterior model expectation, respectively:

\begin{align}
	\gmslctrue &= \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} \thicknesschangeice^\dagger, \label{eq:true-gmsl-model} \\
	\gmslcbay &= \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} \thicknesschangeice^{p}. \label{eq:bay-gmsl-model}
\end{align}


The GMSL altimetry estimate is calculated from the available sea surface height change (SSHC) point measurements, $d_i$, using an area-weighted scheme (\autoref{eq:gmsl_ssh_alt_integral}). Each individual observation is treated as an independent 1D Gaussian measure

\begin{equation}
  d_i \sim \mathcal{N}\left(d_i, \covariance{n,\,i}\right),
\end{equation}

where $\covariance{n,\,i}$ represents the noise variance at that specific point. These individual measures are averaged (with cosine-latitude weights, $w_i$) to form a single global distribution. Mathematically, this estimate represents the true ice state evaluated through the forward operators plus an aggregated noise term,

\begin{align}
 \gmslcalt &= \operatorgmsl{\text{point}} \circ \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadice \left(\thicknesschangeice^\dagger\right) + n_{\text{alt}}, \\
 &\sim \mathcal{N}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i d_i, \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2 \covariance{n,\,i} \right). \label{eq:alt-gmsl-model}
\end{align}

The $z$-score is calculated using the expectation and standard deviations, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ respectively, for both the SSHC proxy method and the inversion method:

\begin{align}
    \zbay &= \frac{\gmslctrue - \mubay}{\sigmabay}, \\[7pt]
    \zalt &= \frac{\gmslctrue - \mualt}{\sigmaalt}.
\end{align}

The inversions were run multiple times to generate a suite of $z$-scores for each method, which can be compared to show the improvement of the Bayesian inversion method over the SSHC proxy method.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity tests}
\notebookcite{thomasholland07InversionSensitivityipynb2026}

With any inverse setup, it is important to understand how sensitive the results are to the various parameters of the method. This is particularly significant for this method, as it relies on a number of assumptions and choices in the construction of the prior model space measure.

To test the inversion, we follow the scheme of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}, testing sensitivity to the ice lengthscale, covariance, and expectation bias parameters of the prior.

We set up an inversion scenario similar to the above, with a fixed true ice state, $m_{\Delta I}^\dagger$. Inversions for the true field were run with different priors generated with the systematically varied parameters of interest, and comparison is made between their results and the true field to assess the method's sensitivity to that parameter. We improve upon the tests of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} by repeating this process for multiple true fields, generated as different samples from the prior, to ensure the sensitivity results are not specific to a particular true field.

In addition to the above tests from \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}, the spatial patterns in the ice thickness prior are investigated. True ice states for both spatially uniform ice change and thickness-dependent change patterns were generated. These were both inverted for using the two types of prior model, resulting in four posteriors, and then compared.

\subsubsection{Joint inversion using ice, firn, and ocean dynamic changes}

The model space is now extended to the direct sum of ice thickness change, firn thickness change, and ocean dynamic topography change, inverted simultaneously. This is motivated by the fact that all three processes contribute to observed SLC and are entangled in altimetry observations, especially when considering the similarity of ice and firn thickness change signals, and so we can leverage them all to reveal their SLC contributions.

The joint inversion exploits the complementary information from each data source by constructing a joint forward operator that maps all three model space parameters to a combined data space of SSH altimetry, ice altimetry, and GRACE observations. As such we take the model space, $m$, to be the direct sum of the ice thickness change, firn thickness change space, and ocean dynamic topography change:

\begin{equation}
	m = \thicknesschangeice \oplus \thicknesschangefirn \oplus \odtchange,
\end{equation}

and to invert for these sources, our data, $d$, is a combination of GRACE gravity data, SSH altimetry data, and ice altimetry data:


\begin{equation}
d = d_{\text{SSH altimetry}} \oplus d_{\text{ice altimetry}} \oplus d_{\text{GRACE}}.
\end{equation}

Each source constrains the model space differently: SSH altimetry constrains ocean dynamic topography through its spatial coverage of the sea surface; ice altimetry constrains the partitioning between ice and firn thickness change; and GRACE constrains the total mass change.

Tide gauge observations were considered as an additional data source and tested in preliminary inversions, but were ultimately excluded. Their inclusion is redundant with SSH altimetry for constraining the sea surface signal, and their integration introduced computational difficulties; we therefore defer their inclusion to future work.

Often, multiple model space parameters map to one data space parameter. For example in this inversion, the SSH altimetry points form the linear equation:

\begin{equation}
	d_{\text{SSH altimetry}} = \operatorpointocean\left( \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \left(\operatorloadice m_{\Delta I} + \operatorloadfirn m_{\Delta F} + \operatorloadwater \odtchange\right) + \odtchange\right)
\end{equation}

The forward operator for the joint inversion can be written as a block matrix, with each element being the mapping of the respective model space parameter to the respective data space parameter:

\begin{align}
\begin{pmatrix}
    d_{\text{SSH}} \\ d_{\text{ice}} \\ d_{\text{GRACE}}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
    \operatorpointocean \operatorsshc \operatorslc \operatorloadice
        & \operatorpointocean \operatorsshc \operatorslc \operatorloadfirn
        & \operatorpointocean\left(\operatorsshc \operatorslc \operatorloadwater + 1\right) \\
    \operatorpointice & \operatorpointice & \mathbf{0} \\
    \operatorgrace \operatorslc \operatorloadice
        & \operatorgrace \operatorslc \operatorloadfirn
        & \operatorgrace \operatorslc \operatorloadwater
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
    \thicknesschangeice \\ \thicknesschangefirn \\ \odtchange
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}

However, this is computationally inefficient, as it requires three independent calls to the fingerprint numerical solver. To optimise this forward operator, we can factorise this matrix into sparse matrices


\begin{equation}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
    \operatorpointocean \operatorsshc & \operatorpointocean & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & \operatorpointice & \operatorpointice \\
    \operatorgrace & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorslc & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbf{1}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorloadice & \operatorloadfirn & \operatorloadwater \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} \\
\mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\thicknesschangeice \\
\thicknesschangefirn \\
\odtchange
\end{pmatrix},\label{eq:factorised_joint}
\end{equation}


see \autoref{sec:joint_operator_factorisation} for full derivation. This reduces each iteration time by a factor of $3\times$.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity tests of joint inversions}

The posterior covariance between ice and firn thickness change is inspected to assess how well the method disambiguates between them and how much uncertainty remains.

The importance of each data source is tested by running the joint inversion with that source removed and comparing the results to the full inversion. This shows how much each source contributes to constraining the model space parameters.
