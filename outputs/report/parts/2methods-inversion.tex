\subsection{Bayesian Inversion}

% Breif introduction

By applying the inversion framework outlined in \autoref{sec:bay}, prior knowledge of SL physics is encoded in prior distributions reflecting the state of the system, yielding an updated posterior belief of the state of the model space based on observations made in the data space.



\subsubsection{Simple inversion}

\notebookcite{thomasholland06SimpleInversionipynb2026}

A simple case for this is to invert for ice thickness change, $\thicknesschangeice$, with a single density, from SSH altimetry, $d$ where the forward problem takes the form

\begin{align}
	d &= \operatoraltimetry{\text{ocean}} \circ \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadice (\thicknesschangeice) + n,\\
	d &= \operator{O}{simple}(\thicknesschangeice) + n, \label{eq:simple_case}
\end{align}

where $\operator{O}{simple}$ is the composed forward operator for this simple case and $n$ represents the altimetry noise. The noise is taken to be an independent Gaussian measure in the dataspace with zero mean and covariance $\covariance{n}$, so

\begin{equation}
	n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \covariance{n}).
\end{equation}

A true ice-thickness field, $\thicknesschangeice^\dagger$,  is drawn as a single sample from the prior measure $\thicknesschangeice$, providing a plausible but non-trivial test case that is, by construction, consistent with our prior assumptions. Synthetic altimetry observations, $\dobs$, are then generated by passing through the forward operator and adding the Gaussian noise:

\begin{equation}
	\dobs = \operator{O}{simple}(\thicknesschangeice^\dagger) + n
\end{equation}

Using the Bayesian inversion framework, we generate the posterior model space measure, $m_{\Delta I}^{p}$, which can be inspected to understand the new inversion method. Such inspections include visual comparison of the posterior model expectation against the true field it is inverting for. Both of these can be pushed forward through the sea level operator to obtain their respective sea level fingerprints for comparison.

The posterior covariance between major sources is also inspected to assess how well the method disambiguates between them.

\subsubsection{Comparison with previous method}
\notebookcite{thomasholland06SimpleInversionipynb2026}

To compare the inversion method's estimate of GMSLC with the altimetry estimate, the $z$-scores are compared of both methods relative to the true GMSLC. We generate a true GMSL value, $\gmslctrue$, and the inversion method's estimate, $\gmslcbay$ by applying the mass balance GMSL operator, $\operatorgmsl{\text{true}}$, to the true ice thickness change field and the posterior model expectation, respectively:

\begin{align}
	\gmslctrue &= \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} \thicknesschangeice^\dagger, \label{eq:true-gmsl-model} \\
	\gmslcbay &= \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} \thicknesschangeice^{p}. \label{eq:bay-gmsl-model}
\end{align}


The GMSL altimetry estimate is calculated from the available sea surface height change (SSHC) point measurements, $d_i$, using an area-weighted scheme (\autoref{eq:gmsl_ssh_alt_integral}). Each individual observation is treated as an independent 1D Gaussian measure

\begin{equation}
  d_i \sim \mathcal{N}(d_i, \covariance{n,\,i}),
\end{equation}

where $\covariance{n,\,i}$ represents the noise variance at that specific point. These individual measures are then combined via a cosine-latitude weighted average (with weights $w_i$) to form a single global distribution. Mathematically, this estimate represents the true ice state evaluated through the forward operators plus an aggregated noise term, yielding the following distribution:

\begin{align}
 \gmslcalt &= \operatorgmsl{\text{point}} \circ \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadice (\thicknesschangeice^\dagger) + n_{\text{alt}} \\
 &\sim \mathcal{N}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i d_i, \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i^2 \covariance{n,\,i} \right) \label{eq:alt-gmsl-model}
\end{align}

The $z$-score is calculated using the expectation and standard deviations, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ respectively, for both the old altimetry method and the new inversion method, to compare the performance of the two methods in estimating the true GMSLC:

\begin{align}
    \zbay &= \frac{\gmslctrue - \mubay}{\sigmabay} \\[7pt]
    \zalt &= \frac{\gmslctrue - \mualt}{\sigmaalt}
\end{align}

The inversions were run multiple times to generate a suite of $z$-scores for each method, which can be compared to show the improvement of the Bayesian inversion method over the SSHC proxy method.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity tests}
\notebookcite{thomasholland07InversionSensitivityipynb2026}

With any inverse setup, it is important to understand how sensitive the results are to the various parameters of the method. This is particularly significant for this method, as it relies on a number of assumptions and choices in the construction of the prior model space measure.

To test the inversion, we follow the scheme of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}, testing sensitivity to the ice lengthscale, covariance, and expectation bias parameters of the prior.

We set up an inversion scenario similar to the above, with a fixed true ice state, $m_{\Delta I}^\dagger$. Inversions for the true field were run with priors generated with the systematically varied parameters of interest, and comparison is made between their results and the true field to assess the method's sensitivity to that parameter.

In addition to the above tests from \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}, we test whether spatial patterns in the ice thickness prior affect the inversion. True ice states for both spatially uniform ice change and thickness-dependent change patterns were generated (see \autoref{sec:ice_thickness_priors}). These were both inverted for using the two types of prior model, resulting in four posteriors, and then compared.

\subsubsection{Joint inversion using ice, firn, and ocean dynamic changes}

The model space is now extended to the direct sum of ice thickness change, firn thickness change, and ocean dynamic topography change, inverted simultaneously. This is motivated by the fact that all three processes contribute to observed SLC and are therefore entangled in altimetry observations, making correct source attribution difficult. The problem is compounded by the similarity of ice and firn thickness change signals, which produce comparable spatial patterns and both affect ice surface height and load-induced sea level fingerprints.

The joint inversion exploits the complementary information from each data source by constructing a joint forward operator that maps all three model space parameters to a combined data space of SSH altimetry, ice altimetry, and GRACE observations, to better constrain and disambiguate between these competing contributions.

We take the model space to be the direct sum of the ice thickness change, firn thickness change space, and ocean dynamic topography change:

\begin{equation}
	m = \thicknesschangeice \oplus \thicknesschangefirn \oplus \odtchange
\end{equation}

To invert for these sources, we combine GRACE gravity data, SSH altimetry data, and ice altimetry data. Each source constrains the model space differently: SSH altimetry constrains ocean dynamic topography through its spatial coverage of the sea surface; ice altimetry constrains the partitioning between ice and firn thickness change; and GRACE constrains the total mass change. We take the data space to be the direct sum of these measurements

\begin{equation}
d = d_{\text{SSH altimetry}} \oplus d_{\text{ice altimetry}} \oplus d_{\text{GRACE}}
\end{equation}

Tide gauge observations were considered as an additional data source and tested in preliminary inversions, but were ultimately excluded. Their inclusion is redundant with SSH altimetry for constraining the sea surface signal, and their integration introduced computational difficulties; we therefore defer their inclusion to future work.

Often, multiple model space parameters map to one data space parameter. For this inversion, the SSH altimetry points form the linear equation:

\begin{equation}
	d_{\text{SSH altimetry}} = \operatorpointocean( \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc (\operatorloadice m_{\Delta I} + \operatorloadfirn m_{\Delta F} + \operatorloadwater \odtchange) + \odtchange)
\end{equation}

The forward operator for the joint inversion can be written as a block matrix, with each element being the mapping of the respective model space parameter to the respective data space parameter:

\begin{align}
\begin{pmatrix}
    d_{\text{SSH}} \\ d_{\text{ice}} \\ d_{\text{GRACE}}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
    \operatorpointocean \operatorsshc \operatorslc \operatorloadice
        & \operatorpointocean \operatorsshc \operatorslc \operatorloadfirn
        & \operatorpointocean(\operatorsshc \operatorslc \operatorloadwater + 1) \\
    \operatorpointice & \operatorpointice & \mathbf{0} \\
    \operatorgrace \operatorslc \operatorloadice
        & \operatorgrace \operatorslc \operatorloadfirn
        & \operatorgrace \operatorslc \operatorloadwater
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
    \thicknesschangeice \\ \thicknesschangefirn \\ \odtchange
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{align}

However, this is computationally inefficient, as it requires three independent calls to the fingerprint numerical solver. To optimise this forward operator, we can factorise this matrix into sparse matrices


\begin{equation}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
    \operatorpointocean \operatorsshc & \operatorpointocean & 0 & 0 \\
    0 & 0 & \operatorpointice & \operatorpointice \\
    \operatorgrace & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorslc & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbf{1}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorloadice & \operatorloadfirn & \operatorloadwater \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} \\
\mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & 0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\thicknesschangeice \\
\thicknesschangefirn \\
\odtchange
\end{pmatrix},\label{eq:factorised_joint}
\end{equation}


see \autoref{sec:joint_operator_factorisation} for full derivation. This reduces each iteration time by a factor of $3\times$.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity tests of joint inversions}

The posterior covariance between ice and firn thickness change is inspected to assess how well the method disambiguates between them and how much uncertainty remains.

The importance of each data source is tested by running the joint inversion with that source removed and comparing the results to the full inversion. This shows how much each source contributes to constraining the model space parameters.
