A simple case for this is setting the model space to be a rotationally invariant ice thickness change with a single density and the data space to be changes in SSH altimetry. This would result in the following form:

\begin{align}
	d &= A_{ssh} \circ SSH \circ SL \circ L_{ice} (m_{\Delta I}) + n
\end{align}

Where $m_{\Delta I}$ is the ice thickness model space, modeled as described in \autoref{}. A true ice-thickness field, $m_{\Delta I}^\dagger$,  is drawn as a single sample from the prior measure $m_{\Delta I}$, providing a plausible but non-trivial test case that is, by construction, consistent with our prior assumptions. Synthetic altimetry observations, $d_{obs}$, are then generated by passing through the forward operator and adding Gaussian noise:

\begin{equation}
	d_{obs} = F m_{\Delta I}^\dagger + n, \quad n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \mathcal{C}_n)
\end{equation}

Where the noise covariance, $\mathcal{C}_n$, reflects the measurement uncertainty of the altimeter.

\subsubsection{Comparison with previous method}
\texttt{See notebook: \citetitle{thomasholland06SimpleInversionipynb2026}}

We collapse the full spatial sea-level fingerprints to a single scalar: the global mean sea level (GMSL) contribution. This is accomplished by applying the GMSL projection operator, a spatially uniform averaging kernel, appropriately area-weighted, to both the posterior distribution and the true field. This generates a true GMSL value, $\Delta GMSL^{true}$, and the inversion method's estimate, $\Delta GMSL^{bay}$:

\begin{align}
	\Delta GMSL^{true} &= G m_{\Delta I}^\dagger \label{eq:true-gmsl-model} \\
	\Delta GMSL^{bay} &= G m_{\Delta I}^{p} \label{eq:bay-gmsl-model}
\end{align}

For comparison, I also computed an old method GMSL estimate obtained by treating the raw altimetry observations as a direct proxy for sea-level change, without any inversion step, as described in \autoref{}. This method propergates through the altimetry error used in our inversion, $n$, with the assumption that this is the only source of error associated with this method or that other sources of error are consistent to both methods and thus moot.

\begin{equation}
  \Delta GMSL^{alt} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\dfrac{1}{\mathbf{Len}[d_{obs}]}\sum^{\mathbf{Len}[d_{obs}]}_{i=1} d_{obs,\,i},\, \mathcal{C}_n\right) \label{eq:alt-gmsl-model}
\end{equation}

The $z$-score can be calculated for each method:

\begin{align}
	z^{bay} &= \dfrac{\Delta GMSL^{true} - \mathbb{E}[\Delta GMSL^{bay}]}{\mathbb{SD}[\Delta GMSL^{bay}]} \\
	z^{alt} &= \dfrac{\Delta GMSL^{true} - \mathbb{E}[\Delta GMSL^{alt}]}{\mathbb{SD}[\Delta GMSL^{alt}]}
\end{align}

The inversions can be run multiple times smth smth repeatability and validates the method. The $z$-score can be averaged and compared across multiple runs.

The model space would be a direct sum of the ice thickness change, firn thickness change space, and ocean dynamic topography change:

\begin{equation}
	m = m_{\Delta I} \oplus m_{\Delta F} \oplus m_{\Delta ODT}
\end{equation}

With the data space being the direct sum of SSH altimetry data, tide gauge data, and ice altimetry data:

\begin{equation}
	d = d_{\Delta SSH} \oplus d_{\Delta TG} \oplus d_{\Delta ISH}
\end{equation}

It is often the case where multiple model space parameters map to one data space parameter, for example in our case, SSH altimetry points forms the linear equation:

\begin{equation}
	d_{\Delta SSH} = P_{S}( SSH \circ F (L_I m_{\Delta I} + L_F m_{\Delta F} + L_W m_{\Delta ODT}) + m_{\Delta ODT})
\end{equation}

Smth smth optimised form as one fingerprint solve, which is the computationally expensive step. However, for forming our forward operator from direct sum, intiialy we can each term in our forward operator direct sum in terms of mapping from one model space to one data space, and then optimise later after conceptualising the process. As such the forward problem can be written as:

\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix}
	\text{SSH Altimetry Points}\\
	\text{Tide Gauge Points}\\
	\text{Ice Altimetry Points}
\end{bmatrix}
&\nonumber\\&=
\begin{bmatrix}
	\mathbf{P_S S F L_I} & \mathbf{P_S S F L_F} & \mathbf{P_S(S F L_W + 1)} \\
	\mathbf{P_T F L_I} & \mathbf{P_T F L_I} & \mathbf{P_T (F L_W + 1)}\\
	\mathbf{P_I} & \mathbf{P_I} & \mathbf{0}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
	\Delta T_\text{Ice}\\
	\Delta T_\text{Firn}\\
	\Delta SSH_\text{OD}
\end{bmatrix}
\end{align}

Then to optimise this to reduce the number of fingerprint calls, we can factorise this matrix into sparse matrices, shown in \autoref{eq:factorised_joint}. This reduced each iteration time, with a speed-up of $3\times$.

\begin{equation}
=
	\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{P_S S} & \mathbf{P_S} & 0 & 0 \\
\mathbf{P_T} & \mathbf{P_T} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{P_I} & \mathbf{P_I}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{F} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbf{1}
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf{L_I} & \mathbf{L_F} & \mathbf{L_W} \\
0 & 0 & \mathbf{1} \\
\mathbf{1} & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \mathbf{1} & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
\Delta T_{\text{Ice}} \\
\Delta T_{\text{Firn}} \\
\Delta SSH_{\text{OD}}
\end{bmatrix}\label{eq:factorised_joint}
\end{equation}
