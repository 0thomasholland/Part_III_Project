To quantify the error of using SSHC as a proxy for GMSLC, and to develop a new method that uses the Bayesian framework, it is necessary to design a suitable model space, data space, and operators that map between these spaces. These must be consistent with the underlying physics and the available data. They are then used to test and quantify the error of the SSHC proxy method. Once this is understood, the Bayesian inversion method can be developed, compared, and tested.

\subsection{Designing the model space, data space, and suitable operators}

Within the mathematical framework (\autoref{sec:maths_background}), we define model spaces and data spaces, and design linear operators that map between these spaces in a way consistent with the underlying physics and the available data.

\subsubsection{Sea Surface Height operator}

\notebookcite{thomasholland01SeaSurface2026}


The numerical solution to the sea-level problem has been implemented in \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} using the recent advancements of \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} related to calculating the adjoint of the sea level fingerprint equation.

As discussed in \autoref{sec:slp}, the relation between SSHC and SLC is

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \slc + \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{\psi}{g} \label{eq:slc_ssh_eq}
\end{equation}

and as the fingerprint code implemented in \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} returns the response of the Earth to a load. This response space is a direct sum containing SLC, displacement, potential change, and angular velocity change, $\slc \oplus \mathbf{u} \oplus \psi \oplus \omega$. Using the response space, it is possible to implement a linear operator that maps to the sea surface height space.

This operator computes the sea surface height change using the radial displacement and angular velocity change, removes rotational contributions, and applies an ocean projection operator to restrict the field to the ocean domain. This can be represented as

\begin{equation}
    \sshc = \operatorsshc(\slc, \mathbf{u}, \omega)
\end{equation}

which can be used as an operator to map from load change to sea surface height change, by first mapping from load change to the response space using the fingerprint operator, and then applying the SSHC operator to map from the response space to the SSHC space as

\begin{equation}
    \sshc = \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc (\text{load})
\end{equation}

where $\operatorslc$ is the fingerprint operator that maps from load change to the response space.

\subsubsection{Point sampling}

\notebookcite{thomasholland05AltimetrySamplingipynb2026}

To build data spaces, we design operators that map from the continuous fields to the discrete observations made by satellite altimeters. Following \citetitle{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025}, we use a linear operator in the form of a Dirac delta function, which samples the input field at given points and returns a vector of sampled values:

\begin{align}
  \operatoraltimetry{.}&: H^s(\mathbb{S}^2) \to \mathbb{R}^N, \\
  (\operatoraltimetry{}\, f)_i &= \delta_{\mathbf{x}_i}(f) = f(\mathbf{x}_i), \quad i = 1, \dots, N
\end{align}

with the condition $s > \frac{n}{2}$ \autocite{rudinFunctionalAnalysis1991}. Points are selected as the subset of a regular latitude-longitude grid $\mathcal{G}(\Delta\phi, \Delta\lambda)$ lying within the relevant domain. We define three point sets for the construction of data spaces:

\begin{align}
  \operatoraltimetry{S} &= \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \Omega_{\text{ocean}} \}, \\
  \operatoraltimetry{I} &= \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \Omega_{\text{ice}} \}, \\
  \operatoraltimetry{alt} &= \{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \Omega_{\text{alt}} \},
\end{align}

where $\Omega_{\text{alt}}$ denotes the ocean domain masked to the satellite altimetry coverage region.


\subsubsection{GMSL operators}

\notebookcite{thomasholland05AltimetrySamplingipynb2026}

We calculate true GMSLC from a simple mass balance from ice change, $\gmslc$, across the ice field domain, $\Omega_I$, accounting for the density difference between ice, $\rho_I$, and water, $\rho_W$. Assuming a constant coastline shape, this takes the form

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc: \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} = \frac{\rho_W}{A_{ocean} \rho_I} \int_{\Omega_I} \thicknesschangeice (\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}). \label{eq:gmsl_integral}
\end{equation}

An SSH-based GMSLC estimate is the surface integral of the SSHC field over the oceans

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\sshc}: \operatorgmsl{\sshc} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega} \sshc(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}), \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_integral}
\end{equation}

where $|\Omega| = \int_{\Omega} \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})$ is the total surface area of the ocean domain, $\Omega$.
A SSH altimetry based GMSLC can be calculated by a surface integral of the SSHC over the oceans within a defined latitudinal range, which represents what is sampled by satellite altimeters:

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\altimetry}: \operatorgmsl{\altimetry} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{\altimetry}|}\int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \sshc(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_alt_integral}
\end{equation}

where $|\Omega_{\altimetry}| = \int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})$ is the total surface area of the ocean within the altimetry coverage domain, $\Omega_{\altimetry}$.

An SSH point-altimetry-based GMSLC estimate is computed by sampling the SSHC field at the altimetry points, which more accurately reflects satellite altimetry practice. A naive arithmetic mean of the form

\begin{equation}
    % \gmslc^{\text{point}}: \operatorgmsl{\text{point, naive}} =
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sshc(\mathbf{x}_i)\label{eq:gmsl_ssh_point_naive}
\end{equation}

is biased whenever the observation points are not uniformly distributed with respect to surface area. On a regular latitude-longitude grid, point density scales as $\cos^{-1}(\lambda)$, so polar regions are overrepresented. A $\cos(\lambda)$-weighted scheme corrects for this by replacing the uniform weights with $w_i = \cos(\lambda_i) / \sum_j \cos(\lambda_j)$, giving the area-weighted point estimator

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\text{point}}: \operatorgmsl{\text{point}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \, \sshc(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad w_i = \frac{\cos(\lambda_i)}{\sum_j \cos(\lambda_j)}. \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_point}
\end{equation}

The full derivation of this quadrature scheme is given in \autoref{sec:point_gmsl_derivation}.

% This weighted estimator converges to $\operatorgmsl{\altimetry}$ as $N \to \infty$ and grid spacing tends to zero, since the discrete sum approximates the spherical surface integral with the correct area element $R^2 \cos(\lambda) \, \mathrm{d}\phi \, \mathrm{d}\lambda$, providing a natural consistency check between the two approaches.

To give lower error bounds for the altimetry-based GMSLC estimate, we adopt a best-case assumption of dense, uniform sampling across the altimetry domain $\Omega$. In this limit, the discrete sum over altimetry measurement points converges to a continuous surface integral so that the estimated GMSLC reduces to the true spatial average of the field:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{|\Omega_{ALT}|}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sshc(\mathbf{x}_i)\, \delta A_i
    \;\xrightarrow{N \to \infty}\;
    \frac{1}{|\Omega_{ALT}|}\int_{\Omega_{ALT}} \sshc(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}) = \gmslc^{\altimetry}\label{eq:altimetry_equation}
\end{equation}

This constitutes a best-case scenario for the estimation error: any realistic altimetry network, with finite and non-uniform spatial coverage, will perform no better than this ideal limit.

\subsubsection{GRACE Gravity Observations}

\citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} includes a GRACE operator which maps from load change to the gravity change observed by the GRACE satellites. The operator extracts the spherical harmonic coefficients of the gravitational potential perturbation $\phi$ from the fingerprint response space, for degrees $2\leq l \leq L$:

\begin{align}
    \operatorgrace:
    (\slc \oplus \mathbf{u} \oplus \phi \oplus \boldsymbol{\omega})
    &\to \mathbb{R}^{(L+1)^2 - 4} \\
    (\slc, \mathbf{u}, \phi, \boldsymbol{\omega})
    &\mapsto \{\phi_{lm}\}_{l=2}^{L}
\end{align}

where $\phi_{lm}$ are the fully normalised, Condon-Shortley-phased spherical harmonic coefficients of the gravitational potential perturbation. Degrees $l < 2$ are excluded as degree 0 corresponds to conservation of mass and degree 1 to geocentre shift. Crucially, $\phi$ is a globally defined field — it is not masked to any one region, like the other observation operators, so it is sensitive to mass changes in the oceans as well as the ice sheets. In this report, the truncation is set to $L=96$, which is the same as the truncation used in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}.

\subsubsection{Modelling Ice and Firn Thickness Change}\label{sec:ice_thickness_priors}

\notebookcite{thomasholland04IceFirnipynb2026}


\begin{figure}[h]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{04_variability_fields_side_by_side.pdf}
	\caption{a}\label{fig:spatial_ice_function}
\end{figure}

The starting point for the error quantification and models is the ice model space, which represents our prior belief about possible ice thickness change. To do this, an initial covariance structure, $\covariance{0}$, can be generated that has decaying spatial correlation, which is a common choice for representing spatial fields, and is consistent with the idea that changes in ice thickness are more likely to be similar at nearby locations than at distant ones. This can be implemented using a heat kernel covariance (\autoref{eq:heat_kernel_covariance}), which has a structure that decays exponentially with distance, and is parameterised by a length scale that controls the rate of decay and a pointwise standard deviation based on an amplitude. To control the amplitude of the field, the standard deviation can be scaled to have a prescribed standard deviation for the implied GMSLC, which is a physically meaningful way to set the prior scale rather than an arbitrary choice. A sensible way to do this is to generate a field to act as the initial ice thickness change

\begin{equation}
	\thicknesschangeice \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \covariance{0})
\end{equation}

which we can then measure equivalent GMSLC variance by applying the GMSL operator to this field

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\gmslc}^2 = \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} \covariance{0} \operatorgmsl{\text{true}}^*
\end{equation}

and then use this to scale the original operator to have the desired GMSLC standard deviation, $\sigma_{\gmslc}^{\text{desired}}$, by applying a scaling factor to the original operator

\begin{equation}
    \thicknesschangeice \sim \frac{\left(\sigma_{\gmslc}^{\text{desired}}\right)^2}{\sigma_{\gmslc}^2} \mathcal{N}(0, \covariance{0})
\end{equation}

which results in the ice thickness covariance, $\covariance{\thicknesschangeice}$, being scaled by the square of the scaling factor

\begin{equation}
\covariance{\thicknesschangeice} \\ = \frac{\left(\sigma_{\gmslc}^{\text{desired}}\right)^2}{\sigma_{\gmslc}^2} \covariance{0}
\end{equation}


\begin{figure}[]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{04_variable_fields_thickness_load_grid.pdf}
	\caption{Firn and ice measures generated from spatially variable fields.
	Firn has been scaled to produce the standard deviation equivalent of 10 mm GMSL; Ice has been scaled to produce the standard deviation equivalent of 20 mm GMSL; with firn having a density 0.4 times that of ice \autocite{ligtenbergImprovedSemiempiricalModel2011}.}\label{fig:ice_samples}
\end{figure}

Studies \autocite[e.g.][]{mcmillanRapidDynamicActivation2014, nilssonElevationChangeAntarctic2022} have shown spatial patterns suggesting that thickness changes that occur near the edges of the ice sheets are more likely to be from denser ice loss, compared to thickness change in more central, thicker locations, which is more likely to be surficial fluctuation in firn \autocite{liModelingFirnCompaction2011}. Firn is the intermediate stage between snow and ice, and is characterised by a lower density than ice, as well as a different response to climate forcing \autocite{ligtenbergImprovedSemiempiricalModel2011}.

To capture this relationship, we define a function $\alpha$ that maps ice thickness to the fraction of ice fluctuation, $\alpha(x)$, versus firn change, ($1-\alpha(x)$) (see \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function}a), which takes the form of a generalised logistic curve \autocite{richardsFlexibleGrowthFunction1959}

\begin{equation}
    \alpha(x) = a + \frac{k - a}{\left(1 + e^{b(x - m)}\right)^{1/\nu}}, \quad T_i \to x \in [0,1] \label{eq:spatial_prob_func}
\end{equation}

where $x$ is a linearly standardised thickness $(x \in [0,1])$, $a$ is the lower asymptote, $k$ the upper asymptote, $b$ the steepness parameter, $m$ the inflection threshold, and $\nu$ the asymmetry parameter. The parameters that control the shape of the curve, $a$, $k$, $b$, $m$, and $\nu$, are set heuristically to reflect known glaciological structure and to match the spatial structure shown in other studies \autocites[e.g. ][]{meminSnowIceheightChange2014, pritchardExtensiveDynamicThinning2009, shepherdTrendsAntarcticIce2019, nilssonElevationChangeAntarctic2022}, and could in future be constrained empirically.

This is applied to two uniform heat kernel fields by applying a point multiplication operator which results in the point-wise variance being scaled by the function

\begin{align}
    \covariance{\thicknesschangeice} &= W \covariance{0} W^*, \quad W = \text{diag}(\alpha(T_i)),\label{eq:spatial_ice_covariance} \\
    \covariance{\thicknesschange{f}} &= \tilde{W} \covariance{0} \tilde{W}^*, \quad \tilde{W} = \text{diag}(1 - \alpha(T_i)). \label{eq:spatial_firn_covariance}
\end{align}

The thickness measures are then scaled using the GMSL scaling equation (\autoref{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}), with suitable densities, to achieve GMSL standard deviations appropriate for ice and firn contributions. \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function} shows the application of this.



To map these two height fields into the load space, we multiply the thickness fields by the respective densities of ice and firn.

\subsubsection{Modelling Ocean Dynamics}
\notebookcite{thomasholland03OceanDynamicsipynb2026}



When considering ocean dynamics, on shorter time scales for example less than a month, the spatial structure of the variance is likely to be more variable, and it is unlikely that there are regions of the ocean that have higher variability than others REF. This could be due to a variety of factors, such as ocean currents, eddies, and other dynamic processes that can lead to spatially heterogeneous patterns of SLC. However, on longer time scales, on month to year or more, it is likely that there are regions of the ocean that have higher variability than others, due to factors such as ocean circulation patterns, topography, and other factors that can lead to spatially heterogeneous patterns of SLC \autocite{koppGeographicVariabilitySeaLevel2015, coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022}.

For the former, a spatially uniform covariance could be used, which would assume that the variance of ocean dynamics is the same across the entire ocean domain. This could be implemented using a heat kernel covariance with a constant pointwise standard deviation, which would result in a spatially homogeneous field of ocean dynamics.

For the latter, a spatially variable covariance could be used, which would allow for different variances in different regions of the ocean. This can be implemented by applying a pointwise scaling function to the covariance.

As an example, to derive spatially variable ocean variance, the DUACS dataset \autocite{copernicusclimatechangeserviceGLOBALOCEANGRIDDED2021} was sub-sampled into the required fields, binned into monthly changes and averaged,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{OD}}^2(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \thicknesschange{OD}^i(\mathbf{x}) - \thicknesschange{OD}^{i-1}(\mathbf{x}) \right)^2,
\end{equation}


then scale the field between 0 and 1 using a min-max scaler

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\sigma}_{\text{OD}}^2(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2(\mathbf{x}) - \min(\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2)}{\max(\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2) - \min(\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2)},
\end{equation}

and apply this as a pointwise scaling function to the covariance of the ocean dynamics field

\begin{equation}
    \covariance{\thicknesschange{OD}} = W \covariance{0} W^*, \quad W = \text{diag}(\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{OD}}(\mathbf{x}))
\end{equation}



resulting in the point-wise standard deviation being scaled by the standard deviation of the ocean dynamics field, which gives a spatially variable covariance structure. This is shown in the top panel of  \autoref{fig:spatial_odt} with samples of the two types of ocean dynamics field shown in the bottom panel.

Ocean dynamics does not result in a change in mass, but rather a redistribution of mass \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}, and so the thickness change must average to zero across the ocean domain in all cases. This constraint is enforced by applying an operator that removes any non-zero mean from the field.

Note that the redistribution of mass due to ocean dynamics affects the equipotential surface. Whenever calculating the fingerprint, we therefore also pass the height change due to ocean dynamics. This results in the total change due to ocean dynamics being the combination of the thickness change and the fingerprint response:

\begin{align}
	\slc &= \thicknesschange{OD} + \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadwater(\thicknesschange{OD}), \\
	\sshc &= \thicknesschange{OD} + \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadwater(\thicknesschange{OD}).
\end{align}
