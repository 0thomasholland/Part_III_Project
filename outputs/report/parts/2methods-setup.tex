To quantify the SSHC proxy error and develop the Bayesian inversion, we design a model space, data space, and connecting operators consistent with the SLC physics and available data, then use them to test the proxy method before developing and validating the inversion method.

\subsection{Designing the model space, data space, and suitable operators}

Within the mathematical framework (\autoref{sec:maths_background}), we define model spaces and data spaces, and design linear operators that map between these spaces in a way consistent with the underlying physics and the available data.

\subsubsection{Sea Surface Height operator}

\notebookcite{thomasholland01SeaSurface2026}


The numerical solution to the sea-level problem has been implemented in \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} using the recent advancements of \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} related to calculating the adjoint of the sea level fingerprint equation.

As discussed in \autoref{sec:slp}, the relation between SSHC and SLC is

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \slc + \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{\psi}{g} \label{eq:slc_ssh_eq}
\end{equation}

and as the fingerprint code implemented in \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} returns the response of the Earth to a load. This response space is a direct sum containing SLC, displacement, potential change, and angular velocity change, $\slc \oplus \mathbf{u} \oplus \phi \oplus \boldsymbol{\omega}$. Using the response space, it is possible to implement a linear operator, $\operatorsshc$, that computes the sea surface height change using the radial displacement and angular velocity change, removes rotational contributions. This can be represented as

\begin{equation}
    \sshc = \operatorsshc\left(\slc, \mathbf{u}, \omega\right)
\end{equation}

and so can be composed with the fingerprint load-to-response operator, $\operatorslc$, to give a direct mapping from load change to SSHC:

\begin{equation}
    \sshc = \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \left(\text{load}\right).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Point sampling}

\notebookcite{thomasholland05AltimetrySamplingipynb2026}

To build data spaces, we design operators that map from the continuous fields to the discrete observations made by satellite altimeters. Following \citetitle{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025}, we use a linear operator in the form of a Dirac delta function, which samples the input field at given points and returns a vector of sampled values:

\begin{align}
  \operatoraltimetry{.}&: H^s\left(\mathbb{S}^2\right) \to \mathbb{R}^N, \\
  \left(\operatoraltimetry{}\, f\right)_i &= \delta_{\mathbf{x}_i}\left(f\right) = f\left(\mathbf{x}_i\right), \quad i = 1, \dots, N
\end{align}

with the condition $s > \frac{n}{2}$ \autocite{rudinFunctionalAnalysis1991}. Points are selected as the subset of a regular latitude-longitude grid $\mathcal{G}\left(\Delta\varphi, \Delta\lambda\right)$ lying within the relevant domain. We define three point sets for the construction of data spaces, where $\operatoraltimetry{S}$ samples the SSHC field at the altimetry grid, $\operatoraltimetry{I}$ samples the ice thickness change field at the altimetry grid, and $\operatoraltimetry{alt}$ samples the SSHC field at the altimetry grid but only within the altimetry coverage region:

\begin{align}
  \operatoraltimetry{S} &= \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \Omega \right\}, \\
  \operatoraltimetry{alt} &= \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \Omega_{\altimetry} \right\},\\
  \operatoraltimetry{I} &= \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{G} \mid \mathbf{x} \in \Omega_I \right\},
\end{align}

where $\Omega_{\altimetry}$ denotes the ocean domain masked to the satellite altimetry coverage region, $\Omega$ denotes the full ocean domain, and $\Omega_I$ denotes the ice field domain.


\subsubsection{GMSL operators}

\notebookcite{thomasholland05AltimetrySamplingipynb2026}

We calculate true GMSLC from a simple mass balance of ice change, $\gmslc$, across the ice field domain, $\Omega_I$, accounting for the density difference between ice, $\densityice$, and water, $\densitywater$. Assuming a constant coastline shape, this takes the form

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc: \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} = \frac{\densityice}{A_{\text{ocean}} \densitywater} \int_{\Omega_I} \thicknesschangeice \left(\mathbf{x}\right)\, \mathrm{d}S\left(\mathbf{x}\right). \label{eq:gmsl_integral}
\end{equation}

An SSH-based GMSLC estimate is the surface integral of the SSHC field over the oceans

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\sshc}: \operatorgmsl{\sshc} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega} \sshc\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\, \mathrm{d}S\left(\mathbf{x}\right), \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_integral}
\end{equation}

where $|\Omega| = \int_{\Omega} \mathrm{d}S\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$ is the total surface area of the ocean domain, $\Omega$.
A SSH altimetry based GMSLC can be calculated by a surface integral of the SSHC over the oceans within a defined latitudinal range, which represents what is sampled by satellite altimeters:

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\altimetry}: \operatorgmsl{\altimetry} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{\altimetry}|}\int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \sshc\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\, \mathrm{d}S\left(\mathbf{x}\right) \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_alt_integral}
\end{equation}

where $|\Omega_{\altimetry}| = \int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \mathrm{d}S\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$ is the total surface area of the ocean within the altimetry coverage domain, $\Omega_{\altimetry}$.

An SSH point-altimetry-based GMSLC estimate samples the SSHC field at the altimetry points. A naive arithmetic mean

\begin{equation}
    % \gmslc^{\text{point}}: \operatorgmsl{\text{point, naive}} =
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sshc\left(\mathbf{x}_i\right)\label{eq:gmsl_ssh_point_naive}
\end{equation}

is biased on a latitude-longitude grid since point density scales as $\cos^{-1}\left(\lambda\right)$; a $\cos\left(\lambda\right)$-weighted scheme corrects this, giving the area-weighted point estimator

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\text{point}}: \operatorgmsl{\text{point}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \, \sshc\left(\mathbf{x}_i\right), \qquad w_i = \frac{\cos\left(\lambda_i\right)}{\sum_j \cos\left(\lambda_j\right)}. \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_point}
\end{equation}

The full derivation of this quadrature scheme is given in \autoref{sec:point_gmsl_derivation}.

% This weighted estimator converges to $\operatorgmsl{\altimetry}$ as $N \to \infty$ and grid spacing tends to zero, since the discrete sum approximates the spherical surface integral with the correct area element $R^2 \cos(\lambda) \, \mathrm{d}\phi \, \mathrm{d}\lambda$, providing a natural consistency check between the two approaches.

To give lower error bounds for the altimetry-based GMSLC estimate, we adopt a best-case assumption of dense, uniform sampling across the altimetry domain $\Omega_{\altimetry}$. In this limit, the discrete sum over altimetry measurement points converges to a continuous surface integral:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{|\Omega_{\altimetry}|}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sshc\left(\mathbf{x}_i\right)\, \delta A_i
    \;\xrightarrow{N \to \infty}\;
    \frac{1}{|\Omega_{\altimetry}|}\int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \sshc\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\, \mathrm{d}S\left(\mathbf{x}\right) = \gmslc^{\altimetry}.\label{eq:altimetry_equation}
\end{equation}

This constitutes a best-case scenario for the estimation error: any realistic altimetry network, with finite and non-uniform spatial coverage, will perform no better than this ideal limit.

\subsubsection{GRACE Gravity Observations}

\citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} includes a GRACE operator which maps from load change to the gravity change observed by the GRACE satellites. The operator extracts the spherical harmonic coefficients of the gravitational potential perturbation, $\phi$, from the fingerprint response space, for degrees $2\leq l \leq L$:

\begin{align}
    \operatorgrace:
    \left(\slc \oplus \mathbf{u} \oplus \phi \oplus \boldsymbol{\omega}\right)
    &\to \mathbb{R}^{\left(L+1\right)^2 - 4}, \\
    \left(\slc, \mathbf{u}, \phi, \boldsymbol{\omega}\right)
    &\mapsto \{\phi_{lm}\}_{l=2}^{L},
\end{align}

where $\phi_{lm}$ are the spherical harmonic expansion coefficients of the gravitational potential perturbation, using the fully normalised Condon-Shortley convention. Degrees $l < 2$ are excluded, as degree 0 corresponds to conservation of mass and degree 1 to geocentre shift. Crucially, $\phi$ is a globally defined field — it is not masked to any one region, like the other observation operators, so it is sensitive to mass changes in the oceans as well as the ice sheets. In this report, the truncation is set to $L=96$, which is the same as the truncation used in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}.

\subsubsection{Modelling Ice and Firn Thickness Change}\label{sec:ice_thickness_priors}

\notebookcite{thomasholland04IceFirnipynb2026}


\begin{figure}[hp]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{png/04_variability_fields_side_by_side.png}
	\caption{The generalised logistic partitioning function $\alpha$ (\autoref{eq:spatial_prob_func}) and its application to the present-day ice thickness field. (Top left) The ice thickness change function $\alpha\left(x\right)$ (solid line) and firn thickness change function $1 - \alpha\left(x\right)$ (dashed line) as a function of ice thickness, evaluated with parameters $a = 0.1$, $k = 0.9$, $b = 10.0$, $m = 0.45$, $\nu = 0.75$. (Top right) Present-day ice thickness field from \citetitle{royICE7G2018} at 0 ka, used as input to $\alpha$. (Bottom left, bottom right) Pointwise prior standard deviation multiplier fields for firn and ice thickness change, respectively, obtained by evaluating $1 - \alpha\left(T_i\right)$ and $\alpha\left(T_i\right)$ at each grid point. This is consistent with the known glaciological structure of the ice sheets, where central, thicker regions are more likely to be in a quasi-dynamic equilibrium with surficial fluctuations in firn, while edge, thinner regions are more likely to be undergoing dynamic thinning.
	}\label{fig:spatial_ice_function}
\end{figure}

The ice model space is designed to represent our prior belief about possible ice thickness change: that changes in ice thickness are more likely to be similar at nearby locations than at distant ones. To capture this, an initial covariance structure, $\covariance{0}$, can be generated that has decaying spatial correlation. This can be implemented using a heat kernel covariance (\autoref{eq:heat_kernel_covariance}), which decays exponentially with distance, and is parameterised by a length scale that controls the rate of decay and a pointwise standard deviation which is based on an amplitude. To control this amplitude of the field, the standard deviation can be scaled to have a prescribed standard deviation for the implied GMSLC, which is a physically meaningful way to set the prior scale rather than an arbitrary choice. A sensible way to do this is to generate a field to act as the initial ice thickness change

\begin{equation}
	\thicknesschangeice \sim \mathcal{N}\left(0, \covariance{0}\right)
\end{equation}

from which we can then measure equivalent GMSLC variance by applying the GMSL operator to this field to get

\begin{equation}
    \sigmagmsl^2 = \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} \covariance{0} \operatorgmsl{\text{true}}^*
\end{equation}

and then use this to scale the original operator to have the desired GMSLC standard deviation, $\sigmatarget$, by applying a scaling factor to the original operator

\begin{equation}
    \thicknesschangeice \sim \frac{\sigmatarget^2}{\sigmagmsl^2} \mathcal{N}\left(0, \covariance{0}\right)
\end{equation}

which results in the ice thickness covariance, $\covariance{\thicknesschangeice}$, being scaled by the square of the scaling factor

\begin{equation}
\covariance{\thicknesschangeice} = \frac{\sigmatarget^2}{\sigmagmsl^2} \covariance{0} \label{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}
\end{equation}

Representative samples of the firn and ice thickness fields generated from these scaled priors are shown in \autoref{fig:ice_samples}.


\begin{figure}[]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{png/04_variable_fields_thickness_load_polar_grid.png}
	\caption{Firn and ice measures generated from spatially variable fields.
	Firn has been scaled to produce the standard deviation equivalent of 10 mm GMSL; Ice has been scaled to produce the standard deviation equivalent of 20 mm GMSL; with firn having a density 0.3 times that of ice (i.e.\ approximately 0.275~g\,cm$^{-3}$) as used throughout this work. Ice prior and firn prior are formed with a length scale of $0.015\times$ and $0.002\times$ Earth's radius respectively (see \autoref{eq:heat_kernel_covariance}). The spatial structure of the fields is inherited from the spatial structure of the covariance, which is modulated by the spatially variable scaling function shown in \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function}. These illustrate the spatial structure of the fields, with ice change more likely in thicker regions and firn change more likely in thinner regions, as well as the relative magnitudes of the two fields. This also shows how the different densities of ice and firn affect the load change, with the same thickness change in ice producing a much larger load change than the same thickness change in firn, resulting in the load change field having a different spatial structure to the thickness change fields.
	}\label{fig:ice_samples}
\end{figure}

Studies \autocite[e.g.][]{mcmillanRapidDynamicActivation2014, nilssonElevationChangeAntarctic2022} have shown spatial patterns suggesting that thickness changes that occur near the edges of the ice sheets are more likely to be from denser ice loss, compared to thickness change in more central, thicker locations, which is more likely to be surficial fluctuation in firn where ice is in a quasi-dynamic equilibrium \autocite{liModelingFirnCompaction2011}. It is useful to model firn as it has a different response to climate forcing compared to ice \autocite{ligtenbergImprovedSemiempiricalModel2011}.

To capture this relationship, we define a function $\alpha$ that maps ice thickness to the amplitude of ice change, $\alpha\left(x\right)$, and firn change, ($1-\alpha\left(x\right)$) (see \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function}). This takes the form of a generalised logistic curve \autocite{richardsFlexibleGrowthFunction1959}

\begin{equation}
    \alpha\left(x\right) = a + \frac{k - a}{\left(1 + e^{b\left(x - m\right)}\right)^{1/\nu}}, \quad T_i \to x \in \left[0,1\right] \label{eq:spatial_prob_func}
\end{equation}

where $x$ is a linearly standardised thickness $\left(x \in \left[0,1\right]\right)$, $a$ is the lower asymptote, $k$ the upper asymptote, $b$ the steepness parameter, $m$ the inflection threshold, and $\nu$ the asymmetry parameter. These parameters are set heuristically to reflect known glaciological structure and to match the spatial structure shown in other studies \autocites[e.g. ][]{meminSnowIceheightChange2014, pritchardExtensiveDynamicThinning2009, shepherdTrendsAntarcticIce2019, nilssonElevationChangeAntarctic2022}, but could in future be constrained empirically based upon prior knowledge or other parameters, such as a function encoding spatial proximity to ice shelves.

This equation is applied to two uniform heat kernel fields by applying a point multiplication operator, prior to GMSLC target scaling, which results in the point-wise variance being scaled by the function

\begin{align}
    \covariance{\thicknesschangeice} &= W \covariance{0} W^*, \quad W = \text{diag}\left(\alpha\left(T_i\right)\right),\label{eq:spatial_ice_covariance} \\
    \covariance{\thicknesschangefirn} &= \tilde{W} \covariance{0} \tilde{W}^*, \quad \tilde{W} = \text{diag}\left(1 - \alpha\left(T_i\right)\right). \label{eq:spatial_firn_covariance}
\end{align}

The thickness measures are then scaled using the GMSL scaling equation (\autoref{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}), with suitable densities, to achieve GMSL standard deviations appropriate for ice and firn contributions. \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function} shows the output covariance fields.


To map these two thickness change fields into the load space, we multiply the thickness fields by the respective densities of ice and firn.

\subsubsection{Modelling Ocean Dynamics}
\notebookcite{thomasholland03OceanDynamicsipynb2026}



On short timescales (less than a month), ocean variability is dominated by transient mesoscale eddies and wind-driven fluctuations, which produce stochastic spatial patterns with no persistent regional structure; in this regime a spatially uniform prior variance is appropriate \autocite{stammerCausesContemporaryRegional2013}. On longer timescales (monthly and above), persistent circulation patterns, basin geometry, and topographic steering give rise to spatially coherent variability, with certain regions consistently more active than others \autocite{koppGeographicVariabilitySeaLevel2015, coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022, stammerCausesContemporaryRegional2013}.

For short timescales, a spatially uniform covariance is used, implemented as a heat kernel with constant pointwise standard deviation. For the longer timescales of relevance to this work, a spatially variable covariance is used, implemented by applying a pointwise scaling function derived from observations to the base covariance.

To derive spatially variable ocean variance, the DUACS dataset \autocite{copernicusclimatechangeserviceGLOBALOCEANGRIDDED2021} was binned into monthly changes and averaged,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{OD}}^2\left(\mathbf{x}\right) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \odtchange^i\left(\mathbf{x}\right) - \odtchange^{i-1}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) \right)^2,
\end{equation}


then the field was scaled between 0 and 1 using a min-max scaler

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\sigma}_{\text{OD}}^2\left(\mathbf{x}\right) = \frac{\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2\left(\mathbf{x}\right) - \min\left(\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2\right)}{\max\left(\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2\right) - \min\left(\sigma_{\text{OD}}^2\right)},
\end{equation}

and apply this as a pointwise scaling function to the covariance of the ocean dynamics field

\begin{equation}
    \covariance{\odtchange} = W \covariance{0} W^*, \quad W = \text{diag}\left(\tilde{\sigma}_{\text{OD}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
\end{equation}



resulting in the point-wise standard deviation being scaled by the standard deviation of the ocean dynamics field, which gives a spatially variable covariance structure. This is shown in the top panel of  \autoref{fig:spatial_odt} with samples of the two types of ocean dynamics field shown in the bottom panel.

Ocean dynamics does not result in a change in mass, but rather a redistribution of mass \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}, and so the thickness change must average to zero across the ocean domain in all cases. This constraint is enforced by applying an operator that removes any non-zero mean from the field.

Note that the redistribution of mass due to ocean dynamics affects the equipotential surface, resulting in the total change due to ocean dynamics being the combination of the thickness change and the fingerprint response:

\begin{align}
	\slc &= \odtchange + \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadwater\left(\odtchange\right), \\
	\sshc &= \odtchange + \operatorsshc \circ \operatorslc \circ \operatorloadwater\left(\odtchange\right).
\end{align}
