For most readers, the mathematical framing given below should be sufficient to understand the remainder of the paper, however for full details for the mathematical framework surrounding the underlying python packages can be found see apendix E of Heathcotte and Al-Attar blah TKTK, which lay outside the scope of this report. % Paragraph: Introduce the function space setting. Explain that fields are  represented as elements of a Sobolev space over the sphere, and why this is appropriate (smoothness, well-defined inner product). Introduce notation.

To represent continuous fields over the sphere (taken to be a unit sphere, $\mathbb{S}^2$, scaled to the dimensions of the Earth) in a mathematically rigorous way, we work within a Sobolev space (\autoref{eq:sobolev}), where the index $s$ controls the smoothness of admissible fields. In practice this means that realisations of the fields are guaranteed to be spatially smooth, which is physically appropriate given the length scales and physical problem involved. For a full explanation see section 2.3.2 of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}.




\begin{equation}\label{eq:sobolev}
    h \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)
\end{equation}

This framework accommodates both deterministic fields, which enter as fixed elements of $H^s(\mathcal{S}^2)$, and Gaussian random fields, which are represented as probability measures over the same space, allowing uncertainty to be propagated consistently through the forward model and for Linear Operators to be functions on both types.

% Paragraph: Introduce linear operators mapping between fields, and their adjoints. This is the machinery used throughout the forward model.

Linear operators map between fields, with their adjoints mapping the inverse action of those operators.



\begin{align}
    A: X &\rightarrow Y \\
    A^*: Y &\rightarrow X
\end{align}

% Paragraph: Introduce Gaussian measures over these spaces. A Gaussian field is characterised by a mean and covariance operator. Connect to the structure -- covariance operators are self-adjoint by definition.

The Gaussian fields over spaces are defined by their mean value and covariance, which in the context of this framework are a vector and linear operator matrix respectively.

\begin{equation}
    \mu = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \mathcal{C}), \quad \mathcal{C} = \mathcal{C}^*
\end{equation}

Linear operators can act on these Gaussians such:

\begin{align}
	A&: X \rightarrow Y\\
	\mu_X &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \mathcal{C}) \in X \\
	A \mu &= \mathcal{N}(A\mathbf{m}, A\mathcal{C}A^*) \in Y
\end{align}

Fields can either be rotationally invariant, such that REF is satsified, or spatially variant, such that there is

\begin{equation}
	f(\mathbf{x'}) = f(\mathbf{Rx}) = f(\mathbf{x})
\end{equation}

As discussed in \autoref{sec:bay}, if there is a scientific

A uniform point value scaled heat kernel gaussian measure is generated across the load space, which is then masked to the ice area. This measure is defined such that the covariance of the measure is scaled such that the standard deviation of the point-wise values are equal to the given amplitude \autocite{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025}.

By scaling the ice thickness change according to \autoref{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}, which acts to scale the ice prior to have the belived standard deviation of the GMSL contirbution of ice melt, this can be satisfactorily used as an ice thickness prior.

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{gmsl}} = \text{std}(G \circ \mu),
    \quad \mathcal{C} \rightarrow \left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma_{\text{gmsl}}}\right)^2 \mathcal{C}
	\label{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}
\end{equation}


However, to consider ice altimetry, the variable density with depth means that thickness change is not linearly related to mass change, and so the simple.

However, as ice altimetry, which is sensitive to thickness change, and ocean fingerprint measurements, through tide gauge and satellite altimetry which are sensitive to ice mass change, could be used to separate out these changes.

To approximate the system, implemented two independently variable fields, one represent ice thickness change and one representing firn thickness change.

Studies (e.g. REF) have use spatial patterns suggesting that thickness changes that occur near the edges of the ice sheets (in the ablation zone) are more likely to be from denser ice loss, compared to thickness change that occurs in more central, thicker, locations are more likely to be surficial fluctuation in firn.

To capture this relationship, a probability map was created that converted from ice thickness to probability of ice and firn melt (see \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function}a), which takes the form of a generalised logistic curve. (\autoref{eq:spatial_prob_func}). Note that parameters are set heuristically to reflect  known glaciological structure and to match the spatial structure shown in other studies \autocites[e.g. ][]{}, and could in future be constrained empirically.


\begin{equation}
    \alpha(h) = a + \frac{k - a}{\left(1 + e^{b(x - m)}\right)^{1/\nu}}, \quad h \to x \in [0,1] \label{eq:spatial_prob_func}
\end{equation}



Where $x$ is the standardised thickness $(x \in [0,1])$, $a$ is lower asymptote, $k$ upper asymptote, $b$ steepness parameter, $m$ inflection threshold, $\nu$ asymmetry parameter.

This is applied to two fields, generated as described previously, by applying a point multiplication operator which results in the point-wise variance being scaled by the probability function (\autoref{eq:spatial_ice_covariance}). The thickness measures are then scaled using the GMSL scaling equation (\autoref{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}), with suitable densities, for GMSLs standard deviations that are believed to be suitable for ice and firn contributions. \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function} shows the application of this.


\begin{align}
    \mathcal{C}_{\text{ice}} &= W \mathcal{C}_0 W^*, \quad W = \text{diag}(\alpha(h))\nonumber\\
    \mathcal{C}_{\text{firn}} &= \tilde{W} \mathcal{C}_0 \tilde{W}^*, \quad \tilde{W} = \text{diag}(1 - \alpha(h))\label{eq:spatial_ice_covariance}
\end{align}

To map these two height fields into the load space, I used the built in operator for ice load, and developed a complimentary operator for firn load.
The load operator is defined in pyslfp as the point-wise multiplication of ice thickness change by the ice density (\autoref{eq:load_ice_op}). For firn, this operator is scaled by the firn density, such that the operator point-wise multiplies the change in firn thickness with the firn thickness.


\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}_{ice}& : L = \rho_{\text{ice}} \odot \Delta h_{\text{ice}}\label{eq:load_ice_op} \\
    \mathcal{L}_{firn}& = \dfrac{\rho_{\text{firn}}}{\rho_{\text{ice}}} \mathcal{L}_{ice} \to \mathcal{L}_{firn}: L =  \rho_{\text{firn}}\odot \Delta h_{\text{firn}}\label{eq:load_firn_op}
\end{align}

A uniform point value scaled heat kernel Gaussian Measure (ASK DAVID) is generated across the load space, which is then masked to the ocean area. This measure is suitable as it allows for the covariance to vary point-wise based on a defined quantity. This means that the field can be scaled to either have uniform standard deviation or to follow observed magnitudes.

Three views could be taken with this prior, either to have a spatially uniform covariance, which could be argued to be valid over shorter time frames, for example considering month to month, where the uncertainty of any spatial pattern is too high, however






As an example, to derive spatially variable ocean variance, DUACS dataset REF downloaded and turned into a scaled field between 0 and 1, EQUATION. This resulted in the point-wise standard deviation being scaled by this factor. See \autoref{fig:spatial_odt}. The benefit of this approach is that when looking at different time frames, it would be possible to take the variance from those time bins, so for example the variance of monthly time gaps, and then take the average of those time bins and build the covariance structure from this. This in theory would give spatial variations on the orders of magnitude and correlation.
