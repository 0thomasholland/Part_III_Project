Then, once this has been understood, the new method can be applied and tested.

\subsubsection{GMSL operators}

True GMSL can be calculated from a simple mass balance from ice change, across the ice field domain $\Omega_I$, accounting for the density difference, and if we assume that the shape of the coastlines is constant, this takes the form:

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc: \operatorgmsl{\text{true}} = \frac{\rho_W}{A_{ocean} \rho_I} \int_{\Omega_I} \thicknesschange{I} (\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:gmsl_integral}
\end{equation}

A SSH based GMSL estimate can be calculated by calculating a surface integral of the SSHC field over the oceans:

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\sshc}: \operatorgmsl{\sshc} = \frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega} \sshc(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_integral}
\end{equation}

Where $|\Omega| = \int_{\Omega} \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})$ is the total surface area of the ocean domain, $\Omega$.
A SSH altimetry based GMSL can be calculated by a surface integral of the SSHC over the oceans within a defined latitudinal range, which represents what is sampled by satelite altimeters:

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\altimetry}: \operatorgmsl{\altimetry} = \frac{1}{|\Omega_{\altimetry}|}\int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \sshc(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x}) \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_alt_integral}
\end{equation}

Where $|\Omega_{\altimetry}| = \int_{\Omega_{\altimetry}} \mathrm{d}S(\mathbf{x})$ is the total surface area of the ocean within the altimetry coverage domain, $\Omega_{\altimetry}$.
Note that the normalisation by $|\Omega_{\altimetry}|$ rather than the full ocean area $|\Omega|$ implicitly assumes that the SSH anomaly outside the altimetry coverage is representative of the global mean, which is an approximation inherent to all altimetry-based GMSL estimates.

A SSH point altimetry based GMSL estimate can be calculated by sampling the SSHC field at the altimetry points. A naive arithmetic mean of the form

\begin{equation}
    % \gmslc^{\text{point}}: \operatorgmsl{\text{point, naive}} =
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \sshc(\mathbf{x}_i)\label{eq:gmsl_ssh_point_naive}
\end{equation}

is biased whenever the observation points are not uniformly distributed with respect to surface area. On a regular latitude-longitude grid, point density scales as $\cos^{-1}(\lambda)$, so polar regions are overrepresented. A $\cos(\lambda)$-weighted scheme corrects for this by replacing the uniform weights with $w_i = \cos(\lambda_i) / \sum_j \cos(\lambda_j)$, giving the area-weighted point estimator:

\begin{equation}
    \gmslc^{\text{point}}: \operatorgmsl{\text{point}} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \, \sshc(\mathbf{x}_i), \qquad w_i = \frac{\cos(\lambda_i)}{\sum_j \cos(\lambda_j)} \label{eq:gmsl_ssh_point}
\end{equation}

This weighted estimator converges to $\operatorgmsl{\altimetry}$ as $N \to \infty$ and grid spacing tends to zero, since the discrete sum approximates the spherical surface integral with the correct area element $R^2 \cos(\lambda) \, \mathrm{d}\phi \, \mathrm{d}\lambda$, providing a natural consistency check between the two approaches. The error assocaited with the point methods (\autoref{eq:gmsl_ssh_point_naive} and \autoref{eq:gmsl_ssh_point}) compared to the integral method (\autoref{eq:gmsl_ssh_alt_integral}) is shown in \autoref{fig:point_alt_errors}.

However, as ice altimetry, which is sensitive to thickness change, and ocean fingerprint measurements, through tide gauge and satellite altimetry which are sensitive to ice mass change, could be used to separate out these changes.

To approximate the system, implemented two independently variable fields, one represent ice thickness change and one representing firn thickness change.

Studies (e.g. REF) have use spatial patterns suggesting that thickness changes that occur near the edges of the ice sheets (in the ablation zone) are more likely to be from denser ice loss, compared to thickness change that occurs in more central, thicker, locations are more likely to be surficial fluctuation in firn.

To capture this relationship, a probability map was created that converted from ice thickness to probability of ice and firn melt (see \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function}a), which takes the form of a generalised logistic curve. (\autoref{eq:spatial_prob_func}). Note that parameters are set heuristically to reflect  known glaciological structure and to match the spatial structure shown in other studies \autocites[e.g. ][]{}, and could in future be constrained empirically.


\begin{equation}
    \alpha(T_i) = a + \frac{k - a}{\left(1 + e^{b(x - m)}\right)^{1/\nu}}, \quad T_i \to x \in [0,1] \label{eq:spatial_prob_func}
\end{equation}



Where $x$ is the standardised thickness $(x \in [0,1])$, $a$ is lower asymptote, $k$ upper asymptote, $b$ steepness parameter, $m$ inflection threshold, $\nu$ asymmetry parameter.

This is applied to two fields, generated as described previously, by applying a point multiplication operator which results in the point-wise variance being scaled by the probability function (\autoref{eq:spatial_ice_covariance}). The thickness measures are then scaled using the GMSL scaling equation (\autoref{eq:ice_gmsl_scaling}), with suitable densities, for GMSLs standard deviations that are believed to be suitable for ice and firn contributions. \autoref{fig:spatial_ice_function} shows the application of this.


\begin{align}
    \covariance{\thicknesschange{i}}    &= W \covariance{0} W^*, \quad W = \text{diag}(\alpha(T_i))\label{eq:spatial_ice_covariance} \\

    \covariance{\thicknesschange{f}} &= \tilde{W} \covariance{0} \tilde{W}^*, \quad \tilde{W} = \text{diag}(1 - \alpha(T_i)) \label{eq:spatial_firn_covariance}
\end{align}

To map these two height fields into the load space, I used the built in operator for ice load, and developed a complimentary operator for firn load.
The load operator is defined in pyslfp as the point-wise multiplication of ice thickness change by the ice density (\autoref{eq:load_ice_op}). For firn, this operator is scaled by the firn density, such that the operator point-wise multiplies the change in firn thickness with the firn thickness.


\begin{align}
    \operatorloadice &= \rho_{i} \odot \thicknesschange{i}\label{eq:load_ice_op} \\
    \operatorloadfirn &= \dfrac{\rho_{\text{firn}}}{\rho_{\text{ice}}}\operatorloadice \implies \operatorloadfirn = \rho_{\text{firn}} \odot \thicknesschange{f}  \label{eq:load_firn_op}
\end{align}
