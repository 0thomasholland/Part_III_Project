% Glacial driven sea level change

This report presents a novel method to understand ice-driven sea level change, centred on using satellite altimetry. Previous methods for understanding sea level change were limited to global estimates of mean sea level change; however, I present methods for more accurately understanding these metrics, but also provides spatial reconstruction of the ice melt that drove the observed sea level change.

Sea Level (\textbf{SL}), also known as Relative Sea Level, is the difference in elevation between the sea surface and the solid earth surface. A change in this elevation difference is termed Sea Level Change (\textbf{SLC}).

Sea Surface Height (\textbf{SSH}), also known as Absolute Sea Level, is defined as the elevation of the sea surface above a reference ellipsoid. Ocean satellite altimetry measures Sea Surface Height, as it measures the height difference between itself on an orbital ellipsoid and the sea surface.

Ice mass change results in SLC, due to the added water volume and the gravitationally consistent sea level change. The latter is the change in sea level that occurs due to the redistribution of mass on the Earth, which affects the gravitational potential and thus the shape of the sea surface. This means that SLC is not uniform across the globe, but rather has a spatial structure that depends on the location of the ice mass change, called a fingerprint \autocite{mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}.

Traditional methods have used satelite surface altimetry to measure SSH change, and then applied a correction to account for the gravitationally consistent sea level change, in order to estimate SLC \autocites[e.g.][]{}.

However, these corrections are based on assumptions and are poorly constrained \autocites[e.g.][]{}, leading to potential significant errors in the estimated SLC. These errors are believed to be significant and locationally variable, meaning that they cannot be accounted for with a simple correction.

By implementing the Bayesian framework, it is possible to improve the accuracy of SLC estimates using satellite altimetry data, and also recover the spatial structure of the ice melt that drove the observed sea level change.

\subsection{Sea level physics in relation to ice sheet change}\label{sec:slp}

I shall be working under the assumptions that the timescales for modern ice-driven sea level change are short enough that the Earth's response to the ice mass change is elastic, after accounting for GIA, and that the fingerprint problem is suitably linear with small changes in sea level resulting in no appreciable change in the shoreline geometry \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}.

For Glacial isostatic adjustment, \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} uses \citetitle{royICE7G2018} \autocite{royICE7G2018} model is used \autocite{royGlacialIsostaticAdjustment2015}

The elastic assumption is broadly valid for modern ice-driven sea level change, as the timescales of interest are on the order of decades to centuries, which is short compared to the timescales of viscoelastic response of the Earth. However, it has been suggested that beneath West Antartica this assumption could fall down due to lower viscosities \autocites{kaufmannLateralViscosityVariations2005}.

SL defines the sea surface as an equipotential surface:

\begin{equation}
	\Phi(\mathbf{x} + \SL \hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \Phi_0 \label{eq:sea_level_equipotential}
\end{equation}

When this is perturbed by an ice thickness change, $\thicknesschangeice$, four things occur simultaneously: the gravitational potential itself changes, by $\phi$; the centrifugal potential changes due to perturbations in the Earth's rotation vector, by $\psi$; the solid Earth surface, $\mathbf{x}$, moves radially, by $\mathbf{u}$; and the value of the potential at the ocean surface shifts, from $\Phi_0$ to $\Phi_0 + \Phi_g$. This results in the SL changing, from $\SL$ to $\SL + \slc$, because the sea surface must adapt to an equipotential of the combined gravitational and centrifugal potentials. This can be captured within the following equation:

\begin{equation}
	(\Phi + \phi + \psi)(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{u} + (\SL + \slc)\mathbf{\hat{r}}) = \Phi_0 + \Phi_g
\end{equation}

If we apply a first order Taylor expansion to the left-hand side, we get:

\begin{equation}
	\Phi(\mathbf{x} + \SL \hat{\mathbf{r}}) + \phi + \psi + \nabla \Phi \cdot (\mathbf{u} + \slc \hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \Phi_0 + \Phi_g
\end{equation}

Which can be simplified: using \autoref{eq:sea_level_equipotential} we can cancel these terms from both sides; $\nabla\Phi$ represents the gravitational acceleration vector with its radial magnitude as $g$, therefore $\nabla\Phi \cdot \mathbf{\hat{r}} = g$; the term $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi$ represents the potential change due to the vertical motion of the solid surface:

\begin{equation}
	\phi + \psi + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi + g \slc = \Phi_g
\end{equation}

Which we can rearrange to get the change in sea level:

\begin{equation}
	\slc = -\frac{1}{g}(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi + \phi + \psi) + \frac{\Phi_g}{g} \label{eq:sl-equation}
\end{equation}

When discussing SSH, we need to account for the fact that SSH is the height of the sea surface above a reference ellipsoid, which is not an equipotential surface. Unlike SL, which is measured relative to the moving solid surface, SSH is measured relative to a fixed reference datum, meaning we must add back the radial displacement of the solid surface. Following the convention of removing the centrifugal contribution, the change in SSH, $\sshc$, is related to the change in SL, $\slc$, by:

\begin{equation}
	\sshc = \slc + u + \frac{\psi}{g} \label{eq:sshc_slc_relation}
\end{equation}

where $u = \hat{\mathbf{r}} \cdot \mathbf{u}$ is the radial displacement of the solid surface. Since $\nabla\Phi$ points approximately radially with magnitude $g$ at the Earth's surface, we have $\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi \approx gu$. Substituting \autoref{eq:sl-equation} into \autoref{eq:sshc_slc_relation}:

\begin{equation}
	\sshc = \underbrace{\left[ -\frac{1}{g}(gu + \phi + \psi) + \frac{\Phi_g}{g} \right]}_{\slc} + u + \frac{\psi}{g}
\end{equation}

The radial displacement terms cancel, as do the centrifugal potential terms, leaving:

\begin{equation}
	\sshc = \frac{\Phi_g}{g} - \frac{\phi}{g} \label{eq:ssh-equation}
\end{equation}

This result is physically intuitive: SSH change has spatial structure through $\phi$, the perturbation to the gravitational potential due to ice mass redistribution, but unlike SL it has no dependence on solid surface displacement.


If we have an ice load, $\zeta$, which is due to the change in ice thickness, $\thicknesschangeice$ with density $\densityice$, then the ice load is:

\begin{equation}
	\zeta = \densityice (1-C) \thicknesschange{I}
\end{equation}

Where $1 - C$ accounts for the possibility of floating ice and C is the ocean function, which is 1 if the point is ocean and 0 if the point is land. This means that the ice load only applies to points where there is no floating ice, as floating ice does not contribute to the load on the solid earth.
