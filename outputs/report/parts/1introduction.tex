This report presents a novel method for understanding ice-driven sea level change using satellite altimetry measurements. Previous methods exploring this area have been limited to global estimates of global mean sea level change. The methods presented here use sea level change measurements from satellite altimeters to reconstruct estimations of the spatial changes in ice thickness that drove those changes. This allows for not only more accurate estimation of the changes in global mean sea level, but also a greater understanding of the overall system.


The sea surface is the time-varying upper boundary of the ocean \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Sea Level (\textbf{SL}), also known as Relative Sea Level, is the difference in elevation between the sea surface and the solid earth surface \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. A change in this elevation difference is termed Sea Level Change (\textbf{SLC}). Sea Surface Height (\textbf{SSH}), also known as Absolute Sea Level, is defined as the elevation of the sea surface above a reference ellipsoid \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Ocean satellite altimetry measures SSH, as it measures the height difference between itself on an orbital ellipsoid and the sea surface \autocite{cheltonChapter1Satellite2001} (see \autoref{fig:ssh_vs_sl}).

Global Mean Sea Level (\textbf{GMSL}) is the spatial average height of SL \autocite{hortonMappingSeaLevelChange2018}, and its change (\textbf{GMSLC}) is influenced by barystatic SLC, changes due to the change of ocean water mass from reservoirs such as ice sheets or land water storage, and steric SLC, changes in ocean density from temperature (thermosteric) or salinity (halosteric) changes \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. GMSLC integrates these effects and so is a fundamental indicator of the Earth's energy imbalance and a key metric for evaluating climate projections \autocite[e.g.][]{slangenEvaluatingModelSimulations2017, shepherdReconciledEstimateIceSheet2012, otosakaMassBalanceGreenland2023}.

However, SLC is highly non-uniform: gravitational, elastic, and rotational effects mean that ice mass loss drives regional SLC that can deviate dramatically from the GMSLC \autocite{mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}, making source attribution and spatial reconstruction of SLC essential for coastal hazard assessment and for the SL budget.

This report focuses on modelling ice-driven barystatic SLC, and so we refer to GMSLC as the global mean barystatic sea level change, and SLC as the local barystatic sea level change, unless otherwise specified.

Mass change in ice sheets results in SLC through four mechanisms: the addition of water volume to the ocean, solid earth deformation proximal to ice change, rotational effects, and the perturbation of the gravitational field. The gravitational redistribution of mass alters the equipotential surface on which SL rests, meaning that SLC is not spatially uniform. This means that the pattern of SLC depends on the location of the ice mass source and exhibits a characteristic spatial structure known as a sea level fingerprint \autocite{farrellPostglacialSeaLevel1976, mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}. \autoref{fig:explainer} schematically shows that proximally to ice melt sea level falls due to less gravitational attraction, and the far sea level rises due to the increased volume.


Until recently, there was difficulty separating ocean dynamic variability from an ice-driven fingerprint signal. However, it has been shown that satellite altimetry contains a detectable ice-driven SLC signal \autocite{coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022}.

Traditional methods for estimating GMSLC from satellite altimeters have used satellite surface altimetry to measure SSH change (\textbf{SSHC}), and then applied an approximate correction to account for the difference between SSH and SL \autocites[e.g.][]{neremEstimatingMeanSea2010, peltierClosureBudgetGlobal2009}; this is referred to here as the \textbf{SSHC proxy method}. These corrections rest on loose assumptions and are poorly constrained \autocite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018, tamisieaOngoingGlacialIsostatic2011}, producing locationally variable errors that no single scalar factor can remove.

\Textcite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018} and others \autocite[e.g.][]{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} have quantified these errors, finding GMSLC biases exceeding 15\% depending on the relative contributions from Greenland and Antarctic ice flux. This report extends the error quantification to explore how uncertainty grows when using SSHC data derived from satellite altimetry to approximate SLC.

The new method presented here avoids these errors by using the full gravitationally consistent SLC physics within the framework of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}. \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} have shown that the use of precise mathematical methods to incorporate the full physics of SLC into an inverse problem framework allows for more accurate reconstruction of ice thickness change from SL observations. The method within this report gives access to the spatial structure of the ice change and the resulting SLC, which is not possible with traditional altimetry methods. A Bayesian framework is used, which incorporates prior information about the system and quantifies uncertainty in the inversion. Error propagation is not possible with traditional GMSLC methods, nor easily done with general inverse methods, but is a natural part of the Bayesian framework.  \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} have shown success in applications of this framework with tide gauge and Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (\textbf{GRACE}) gravity data to reconstruct ice thickness change.

\Textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} recently demonstrated a proof-of-concept Bayesian inversion of satellite altimetry data to recover Greenland ice mass changes over the period 1993--2019, representing an important step towards using the full fingerprint physics in GMSL estimation. However, their approach discretises the Greenland Ice Sheet into six spatially uncorrelated blocks, with an additional uniform shift parameter accounting for far-field contributions. This parameterisation encodes a physically arbitrary spatial structure and assumes independence a priori. A more principled approach is taken in this report to formulate the inverse problem directly on an infinite-dimensional function space, specifying a spatially correlated Gaussian prior over a continuous ice mass field. This avoids the dependence of the posterior on an arbitrary choice of basis and allows spatial correlations in the ice mass field to be encoded in a physically motivated way, yielding a more coherent probabilistic treatment as the model complexity is varied \autocite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}. Furthermore, while \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} pre-correct their observations for ocean dynamic effects prior to inversion.

This work incorporates ocean dynamic topography directly into the model space and inverts for this jointly alongside ice and firn thickness change (densities taken as approximately 0.917 and 0.275~g\,cm$^{-3}$ respectively; with firn representing both the porous layer of compacted snow above the ice and seasonal snow layers). Since firn and solid ice differ markedly in density, an identical ice surface height change can correspond to very different mass changes depending on which layer is responsible, making source attribution ambiguous without first disentangling the two contributions \autocite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026, coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026}. By exploiting both mass sensitive data, GRACE and SLC, as well as volume sensitive data, ice altimetry, joint inversions implemented in this report provide a more principled treatment of these competing signals and better-quantified uncertainty in the resulting SLC reconstruction.

\subsection{Sea level physics in relation to ice sheet change}\label{sec:slp}

Two simplifying assumptions underpin the sea-level problem for calculating SLC and SSHC. First, that the timescales of contemporary ice-driven SLC are short enough that the solid Earth responds elastically to ice mass changes, once glacial isostatic adjustment (\textbf{GIA}) has been accounted for. Second, that the fingerprint problem is adequately linear, such that the small SLC involved produces no significant change in shoreline geometry \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}.

GIA is accounted for within \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} using \citetitle{royICE7G2018} \autocite{royICE7G2018} from \textcite{royGlacialIsostaticAdjustment2015}. The elastic assumption is broadly valid for modern ice-driven SLC, as the timescales of interest are on the order of decades to centuries, which is short compared to the timescales of viscoelastic response of the Earth \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023, coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022, zwallyMassBalanceAntarctic2021}. However, lower viscosities beneath West Antarctica \autocite{kaufmannLateralViscosityVariations2005} mean this assumption may break down there, but this is an area of current research and not fully understood.

Following the assumptions and derivation detailed in \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations}, which follow \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}, let $\Phi$ be the gravitational potential, $\mathbf{x}$ the position vector, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ the unit radial vector, and $\Phi_0$ the constant equipotential value at the sea surface. SL is then defined by

\begin{equation}
    \Phi\left(\mathbf{x}+\SL\hat{\mathbf{r}}\right) = \Phi_0.
\end{equation}

Let $\mathbf{u}$ be the solid Earth displacement, $\phi$ the perturbation to the gravitational potential due to the change in mass distribution, $\psi$ the perturbation to the centrifugal potential due to polar wander, and $\Phi_g$ the shift in the reference equipotential, fixed by conservation of mass. Under suitably small changes to ice thickness (see derivation), SLC is given by

\begin{equation}
  \slc = -\frac{1}{g}\left(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi + \phi + \psi\right)
  + \frac{\Phi_g}{g}.
\end{equation}

Under the further approximation that $\nabla\Phi$ is radial at the Earth's surface, and following the convention of \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} of removing the centrifugal contribution so as to isolate the mass redistribution signal (see \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations} for details), SSHC is related to SLC as

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \slc + \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{\psi}{g},
\end{equation}

which is equivalent to

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \frac{\Phi_g}{g} - \frac{\phi}{g}.
\end{equation}

This result is physically significant: SSHC depends only on the perturbation to the gravitational potential $\phi$, with no dependence on solid surface displacement, $\mathbf{u}$, or centrifugal potential perturbation, $\psi$. This distinction between SLC and SSHC is central to both the systematic errors investigated in this work and to building operators that measure the effects of ice sheet change on the sea surface using satellite altimetry.
