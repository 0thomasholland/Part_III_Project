Calculating ice sheet change from the change in ocean altimetry data is an inverse problem. The choice to use a Bayesian inference and is used to solve this inverse problem.

A Bayesian view of probability differs from a frequentists' perspective. Frequentist statistics interprets probability as long-run frequency, treating parameters as fixed and data as random, using $p$-values and confidence intervals to accept/reject hypotheses. Conversely, Bayesian statistics treats parameters as random, incorporating prior beliefs and updating them with data to produce a posterior distribution, allowing for direct probability statements about hypotheses. This has the benefit that the posterior distributions give quantifications about the uncertainties of the view of world produced by the method, and that, given suitable uncertainties are quantified within our prior, we can directly understand the uncertainties of our results.

Bayesian inference is framed as using a specific implementation of Bayes' theorem:

\begin{equation}
	p(\text{model} | \text{data}) = \dfrac{p(\text{data} | \text{model})p(\text{model})}{p(\text{data})}
\end{equation}

Where the data is the observed data that we collect, or synthetically generate when developing the methods, and the model is the parameters we define across the model space.

Bayesian inversion is the specific application of this framework to inverse problems, where, rather than seeking a single best-fit solution, it characterizes the entire probability distribution of unknown physical parameters from noisy data. To summarise in a simple case, if we state our prior knowledge of a model space in terms of a Gaussian measure given as:

\begin{equation*}
	m \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}, \mathcal{C}_m)
\end{equation*}

Which is suitably pushed forward through our physical system and measured in the data space using a combined forward operator $A$, with some random Gaussian noise associated with the measurements, given as a vector, $n$, with expectation $\bar{n}$ and covariance $\mathcal{C}_n$. This would result in the data space vector, $d$, being:

\begin{align*}
	d &= Am +z\\
	\to  d &\sim \mathcal{N}(A\bar{m} + \bar{n},\quad A\mathcal{C}_mA^* + \mathcal{C}_n)
\end{align*}

Using the form of Bayes' theorem implemented in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} originally presented in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}, the posterior measure of our updated view of the model space given an observation, $d_{obs}$, of the data space would be:

\begin{align}
	m_p = \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}+K(d_{obs}-A\bar{m}-\bar{n},\quad (1-KA)\mathcal{C}_{m}))
\end{align}

Where $K$ is the Kalman gain, which is the information gained by the observation, is given as:

\begin{equation}
	K = \mathcal{C}_{m}A^*(A\mathcal{C}_{m}A^*+\mathcal{C}_{n})^{-1}
\end{equation}
