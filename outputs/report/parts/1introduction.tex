This report presents a novel method for understanding ice-driven sea level change using satellite altimetry measurements. Previous methods exploring this area have been limited to global estimates of global mean sea level change. The methods presented here use sea level change measurements from satellite altimeters to reconstruct estimations of the spatial changes in ice thickness that drove those changes. This allows for not only more accurate estimation of the changes in global mean sea level, but also a greater understanding of the overall system.

The sea surface is the time-varying upper boundary of the ocean \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Sea Level (\textbf{SL}), also known as Relative Sea Level, is the difference in elevation between the sea surface and the solid earth surface \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. A change in this elevation difference is termed Sea Level Change (\textbf{SLC}). Sea Surface Height (\textbf{SSH}), also known as Absolute Sea Level, is defined as the elevation of the sea surface above a reference ellipsoid \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Ocean satellite altimetry measures SSH, as it measures the height difference between itself on an orbital ellipsoid and the sea surface \autocite{cheltonChapter1Satellite2001} (see \autoref{fig:ssh_vs_sl}).

Global Mean Sea Level (\textbf{GMSL}) is the spatial average height of SL \autocite{hortonMappingSeaLevelChange2018}, and its change is influenced by barystatic SLC, due to the change of ocean water mass from reservoirs such as ice sheets or land water storage, and steric SLC, due to changes in ocean density from temperature (thermosteric) or salinity (halosteric) changes \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}.

GMSLC integrates the effects of ocean thermal expansion, land ice melt, and terrestrial water storage changes. It is a fundamental indicator of the Earth's energy imbalance and a key metric for evaluating climate projections \autocite[e.g.][]{slangenEvaluatingModelSimulations2017, shepherdReconciledEstimateIceSheet2012, otosakaMassBalanceGreenland2023}.

However, SLC is highly non-uniform: gravitational, elastic, and rotational effects mean that ice mass loss drives regional SLC that can deviate dramatically from the global mean \autocite{mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}, making source attribution and spatial reconstruction of SLC essential for coastal hazard assessment and for the SL budget.

This report focuses on modelling ice-driven barystatic SLC, and so we refer to GMSLC as the global mean barystatic sea level change, and SLC as the local barystatic sea level change, unless otherwise specified.

Mass change in ice sheets results in SLC through two mechanisms: the addition of water volume to the ocean, and the perturbation of the gravitational field. The gravitational redistribution of mass alters the equipotential surface on which sea level rests, meaning that SLC is not spatially uniform. This means that the pattern of SLC depends on the location of the ice mass source and exhibits a characteristic spatial structure known as a sea level fingerprint \autocite{farrellPostglacialSeaLevel1976, mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}. \autoref{fig:explainer} schematically shows that proximally to ice melt sea level falls due to less gravitational attraction, and the far sea level rises due to the increased volume.

Until recently, the sparse measurements made it difficult to separate ocean dynamic variability from an ice-driven fingerprint signal. However, it has been shown that satellite altimetry contains a detectable ice-driven SLC signal \autocite{coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022}.

Traditional methods for estimating GMSLC from satellite altimeters have used satellite surface altimetry to measure SSH change (\textbf{SSHC}), and then applied an approximate correction to account for the difference between SSH and SL \autocites[e.g.][]{neremEstimatingMeanSea2010, peltierClosureBudgetGlobal2009}; this is referred to here as the \textbf{SSHC proxy method}. However, these corrections are based on loose assumptions and are poorly constrained \autocite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018, tamisieaOngoingGlacialIsostatic2011}, leading to potentially significant errors in the estimated SLC. These errors are locationally variable, meaning they cannot be accounted for with a single scalar correction.

\Textcite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018} and others \autocite[e.g.][]{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} have shown that there are errors associated with this method and made steps to quantify these errors. \textcite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018} suggest that these errors are significant; the bias in GMSLC estimates could exceed 15\%, depending on the relative contributions from Greenland and Antarctic ice mass flux. This report extends the error quantification to explore how uncertainty grows when using SSHC data derived from satellite altimetry to approximate SLC.

The method presented here avoids these errors by using the full gravitationally consistent SLC physics within the framework of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}. \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} have shown that the use of precise mathematical methods to incorporate the full physics of SLC into an inverse problem framework allows for more accurate reconstruction of ice thickness change from sea level observations. The method within this report gives access to the spatial structure of the ice change and the resulting SLC, which is not possible with traditional altimetry methods, rather than relying on approximate corrections.

This work explores modern ice-driven SLC using the full account of the physics related to gravitationally consistent SLC in a mathematically rigorous infinite-dimensional setting. The ice change that drove the observed SLC is solved for using an inverse problem that accounts for SL physics. A Bayesian framework is used, which incorporates prior information about the system and quantifies uncertainty in the inversion. Error propagation is not possible with traditional GMSLC methods, nor easily done with general inverse methods, but is a natural part of the Bayesian framework. In addition, \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} have shown applications of this framework with tide gauge and Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (\textbf{GRACE}) gravity data to reconstruct ice thickness change.


\Textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} recently demonstrated a proof-of-concept Bayesian inversion of satellite altimetry data to recover Greenland ice mass changes over the period 1993--2019, representing an important step towards using the full fingerprint physics in GMSL estimation. However, their approach discretises the Greenland Ice Sheet into a small number of model parameters and treats these as uncorrelated. As discussed in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}, this can introduce artefacts and overly constrained posteriors arising from the discretisation itself rather than from the data. As an improvement, the method operates in a rigorously defined infinite-dimensional setting using spatially correlated Gaussian priors over continuous fields, which avoids these discretisation artefacts and results in a more physically motivated posterior.

Furthermore, while \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} pre-correct their observations for ocean dynamic effects prior to inversion, this work incorporates ocean dynamic topography directly into the model space and inverts for this jointly alongside ice (density taken as 0.917~g\,cm$^{-3}$) and firn (density taken as 0.3 times that of ice, approximately 0.275~g\,cm$^{-3}$; representing both the porous layer of compacted snow above the ice and seasonal snow layers) thickness change. A long-standing challenge in jointly exploiting mass-sensitive observations, such as GRACE or altimetry-derived sea level fingerprints, alongside ice altimetry data is that the latter records volume change rather than mass change. Since firn and solid ice differ markedly in density, an identical ice surface height change can correspond to very different mass changes depending on which layer is responsible, making source attribution ambiguous without first disentangling the two contributions \autocite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026, coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026}. The joint inversions implemented in this report provide a more principled treatment of these competing signals and better-quantified uncertainty in the resulting SLC reconstruction.

\subsection{Sea level physics in relation to ice sheet change}\label{sec:slp}

We work under two assumptions for calculating SLC, termed the sea-level problem, and subsequently SSHC: that the timescales for modern ice-driven SLC are short enough that the Earth's response to the ice mass change is elastic, after accounting for glacial isostatic adjustment (\textbf{GIA}); and that the fingerprint problem is suitably linear, with small SLC resulting in no appreciable change in the shoreline geometry \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}.

GIA is accounted for within \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} using \citetitle{royICE7G2018} \autocite{royICE7G2018} from \textcite{royGlacialIsostaticAdjustment2015}. The elastic assumption is broadly valid for modern ice-driven SLC, as the timescales of interest are on the order of decades to centuries, which is short compared to the timescales of viscoelastic response of the Earth \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023, coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022, zwallyMassBalanceAntarctic2021}. However, lower viscosities beneath West Antarctica \autocite{kaufmannLateralViscosityVariations2005} mean this assumption may break down there.

Following the assumptions and derivation detailed in \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations}, which follow \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}, let $\Phi$ be the gravitational potential, $\mathbf{x}$ the position vector, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ the unit radial vector, and $\Phi_0$ the constant equipotential value at the sea surface. SL is then defined by

\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{x}+\SL\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \Phi_0.
\end{equation}

Let $\mathbf{u}$ be the solid Earth displacement, $\phi$ the perturbation to the gravitational potential due to the change in mass distribution, $\psi$ the perturbation to the centrifugal potential due to polar wander, and $\Phi_g$ the shift in the reference equipotential, fixed by conservation of mass. Under suitably small changes to ice thickness (see derivation), SLC is given by

\begin{equation}
  \slc = -\frac{1}{g}(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi + \phi + \psi)
  + \frac{\Phi_g}{g}.
\end{equation}

Under the further approximation that $\nabla\Phi$ is radial at the Earth's surface, and following the convention of \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} of removing the centrifugal contribution so as to isolate the mass redistribution signal (see \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations} for details), SSHC is related to SLC as

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \slc + \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{\psi}{g},
\end{equation}

which is equivalent to

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \frac{\Phi_g}{g} - \frac{\phi}{g}.
\end{equation}

This result is physically significant: SSHC depends only on the perturbation to the gravitational potential $\phi$, with no dependence on solid surface displacement, $\mathbf{u}$, or centrifugal potential perturbation, $\psi$. This distinction between SLC and SSHC is central to both the systematic errors investigated in this work and to building operators that measure the effects of ice sheet change on the sea surface using satellite altimetry.
