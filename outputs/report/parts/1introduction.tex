In this report we present a novel method that can be used to understand ice-driven sea level change, using satellite altimetry measurements. Previous methods exploring this area have been limited to global estimates of mean sea level change. Here we present methods that use sea-level change measurements from satellite altimeters to reconstruct estimations of the spatial changes in ice thickness that drove those changes. This allows for not only more accurate estimation of mean sea level, but also a greater understanding of the overall system.

The sea surface is the time-varying upper boundary of the ocean \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Sea Level (\textbf{SL}), also known as Relative Sea Level, is the difference in elevation between the sea surface and the solid earth surface \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. A change in this elevation difference is termed Sea Level Change (\textbf{SLC}). Sea Surface Height (\textbf{SSH}), also known as Absolute Sea Level, is defined as the elevation of the sea surface above a reference ellipsoid \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Ocean satellite altimetry measures SSH, as it measures the height difference between itself on an orbital ellipsoid and the sea surface \autocite{cheltonChapter1Satellite2001}.

Global Mean Sea Level (\textbf{GMSL}) is the spatial averaged height of SL \autocite{hortonMappingSeaLevelChange2018}, and its change is influenced by barystatic SLC, due to the change of ocean water mass from reservoirs such as ice sheets or land water storage, and steric SLC, due to changes in ocean density from temperature (thermosteric) or salinity (halosteric) changes \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}.

GMSL change integrates the effects of ocean thermal expansion, land ice melt, and terrestrial water storage changes. It is a fundamental indicator of the Earth's energy imbalance and a key metric for evaluating climate projections \autocite[e.g.][]{slangenEvaluatingModelSimulations2017, shepherdReconciledEstimateIceSheet2012, otosakaMassBalanceGreenland2023}.

However, sea level change is highly non-uniform; gravitational, elastic, and rotational effects mean that ice mass loss drives regional sea level changes that can deviate dramatically from the global mean \autocite{mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}, making source attribution and spatial reconstruction essential for coastal hazard assessment and for the sea level budget.

This report focuses on modelling ice-driven barystatic SLC, and so we refer to GMSLC as the global mean barystatic sea level change, and SLC as the local barystatic sea level change, unless otherwise specified.

Mass change in ice sheets results in SLC through two mechanisms: the addition of water volume to the ocean, and the perturbation of the gravitational field. The gravitational redistribution of mass alters the equipotential surface on which sea level rests, meaning that SLC is not spatially uniform. Instead, the pattern of sea level change depends on the location of the ice mass source and exhibits a characteristic spatial structure known as a sea level fingerprint \autocite{farrellPostglacialSeaLevel1976, mitrovicaRobustnessPredictionsSea2011}, see \autoref{fig:explainer}.
Satellite altimetry contains a detectable ice-driven SLC signal \autocite{coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022}. Until recently, the sparse measurements made it difficult to separate ocean dynamic variability from an ice-driven fingerprint signal.

Traditional methods for estimating GMSLC from satellite altimeters have used satellite surface altimetry to measure SSH change (\textbf{SSHC}), and then applied an approximate correction to account for the difference between SSH and SL \autocites[e.g.][]{neremEstimatingMeanSea2010, peltierClosureBudgetGlobal2009}. However, these corrections are based on loose assumptions and are poorly constrained \autocite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018, tamisieaOngoingGlacialIsostatic2011}, leading to potentially significant errors in the estimated SLC. These errors are locationally variable, meaning they cannot be accounted for with a single scalar correction.

\Textcite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018} and others \autocite[e.g.][]{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} have shown that there are errors associated with this method and made steps to quantify these errors. \textcite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018} suggest that these errors are significant; the bias in GMSLC estimates could exceed 15\% depending on the relative contributions from Greenland and Antarctic ice mass flux. We also extend the error quantification to explore how uncertainty grows when using SSHC data derived from satellite altimetry to approximate SLC.

Our method avoids these errors by using the full gravitationally consistent SLC physics within the framework of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}. Rather than relying on approximate corrections, it gives access to the spatial structure of the ice change and the resulting SLC, which is not possible with traditional altimetry methods.

We explore modern ice-driven sea level change using the full account of the physics related to the gravitationally consistent SLC in a mathematically rigorous infinite-dimensional setting. We solve for the ice change that drove the observed sea level change using an inverse problem that accounts for SL physics. We use a Bayesian framework, which incorporates prior information about the system and quantifies uncertainty in the inversion. Error propagation is not possible with traditional GMSLC methods, nor easily done with general inverse methods, but is a natural part of the Bayesian framework. \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} have shown applications of this framework with tide gauge and Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (\textbf{GRACE}) data to reconstruct ice thickness change.


\Textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} recently demonstrated a proof-of-concept Bayesian inversion of satellite altimetry data to recover Greenland ice mass changes over the period 1993--2019, representing an important step towards using the full fingerprint physics in GMSL estimation. However, their approach discretises the Greenland Ice Sheet into a small number of model parameters and treats these as uncorrelated. As discussed in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}, this can introduce artefacts and overly constrained posteriors arising from the discretisation itself rather than from the data. As an improvement, our method operates in a rigorously defined infinite-dimensional setting using spatially correlated Gaussian priors over continuous fields, which avoids these discretisation artefacts and results in a more physically motivated posterior.

Furthermore, while \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} pre-correct their observations for ocean dynamic effects prior to inversion, we incorporate ocean dynamic topography directly into the model space and invert for it jointly alongside ice and firn thickness change, providing a more principled treatment of these competing signals and better-quantified uncertainty in the resulting sea level reconstruction.

We extend the Bayesian framework to a joint inversion, combining satellite altimetry and ice altimetry data simultaneously to disentangle the contributions of ice melt, firn thickness change, and ocean dynamic topography to the observed sea level signal. This addresses a long-standing difficulty: incorporating mass-sensitive observations, such as GRACE or altimetry-derived SL fingerprints, with ice altimetry data is complicated by the variable density of ice sheet depth profiles, which creates a non-trivial relationship between ice thickness change and the mass change that drives sea level change \autocite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026, coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026}.





\subsection{Sea level physics in relation to ice sheet change}\label{sec:slp}

We work under two assumptions: that the timescales for modern ice-driven sea level change are short enough that the Earth's response to the ice mass change is elastic, after accounting for glacial isostatic adjustment (\textbf{GIA}); and that the fingerprint problem is suitably linear, with small changes in sea level resulting in no appreciable change in the shoreline geometry \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}.

For GIA, \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} uses \citetitle{royICE7G2018} \autocite{royICE7G2018} from \textcite{royGlacialIsostaticAdjustment2015}. The elastic assumption is broadly valid for modern ice-driven sea level change, as the timescales of interest are on the order of decades to centuries, which is short compared to the timescales of viscoelastic response of the Earth \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023, coulsonDetectionSeaLevel2022, zwallyMassBalanceAntarctic2021}. However, lower viscosities beneath West Antarctica \autocite{kaufmannLateralViscosityVariations2005} mean this assumption may break down there.

Following the assumptions and derivation detailed in \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations}, which follow \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}, let $\Phi$ be the gravitational potential, $\mathbf{x}$ the position vector, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ the unit radial vector, and $\Phi_0$ the constant equipotential value at the sea surface. SL is then defined by

\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{x}+\SL\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \Phi_0.
\end{equation}

Let $\mathbf{u}$ be the solid Earth displacement, $\phi$ the perturbation to the gravitational potential due to the change in mass distribution, $\psi$ the perturbation to the centrifugal potential due to polar wander, and $\Phi_g$ the shift in the reference equipotential, fixed by conservation of mass. Under suitably small changes to ice thickness (see derivation), SLC is given by

\begin{equation}
  \slc = -\frac{1}{g}(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi + \phi + \psi)
  + \frac{\Phi_g}{g}
\end{equation}

SSHC is measured relative to a reference ellipsoid rather than the solid Earth surface. Under the further approximation that $\nabla\Phi$ is radial at the Earth's surface, and following the convention of \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} of removing the centrifugal contribution so as to isolate the mass redistribution signal (see \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations} for details), SSHC simplifies to

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \frac{\Phi_g}{g} - \frac{\phi}{g}
\end{equation}

which is equivalent to

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \slc + \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{\psi}{g}.
\end{equation}

This result is physically significant: SSHC depends only on the perturbation to the gravitational potential $\phi$, with no dependence on solid surface displacement, $\mathbf{u}$, or centrifugal potential perturbation, $\psi$. This distinction between SLC and SSHC is central to both the systematic errors investigated in this work and to building operators that measure the effects of ice sheet change on the sea surface using satellite altimetry.
