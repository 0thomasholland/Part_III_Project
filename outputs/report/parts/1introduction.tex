This report presents a novel method to understand ice-driven sea level change, centred on using satellite altimetry. Previous methods for understanding sea level change using satellite altimetry were limited to global estimates of mean sea level change; however, I present methods for more accurately understanding these metrics which also provides spatial reconstruction of the ice melt that drove the observed sea level change.

The sea surface is the time-varying upper boundary of the ocean \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Sea Level (\textbf{SL}), also known as Relative Sea Level, is the difference in elevation between the sea surface and the solid earth surface \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. A change in this elevation difference is termed Sea Level Change (\textbf{SLC}). Sea Surface Height (\textbf{SSH}), also known as Absolute Sea Level, is defined as the elevation of the sea surface above a reference ellipsoid \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. Ocean satellite altimetry measures SSH, as it measures the height difference between itself on an orbital ellipsoid and the sea surface.

Global Mean Sea Level (\textbf{GMSL}) is the areal average height of SL \autocite{hortonMappingSeaLevelChange2018}. GMSL change (\textbf{GMSLC}) is influenced by Barystatic and Steric sea-level change. Barystatsic SLC is due to the change of ocean water mass due to flux from water mass reservoirs either in land area, for example ice sheets or land water storage, or in the atmosphere. Streic sea level change is due to the change in ocean density, which can be due to changes in temperature (thermosteric) or salinity (halosteric). \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}

Eustatic SLC is often used in geological context as regarding global-mean sea-level rise or global-mean geocentric sea-level rise, compared to use as a synonym for barystatic within oceanographic contexts \autocite{gregoryConceptsTerminologySea2019}. This report is based around ice driven barystatic SLC, and will refer to GMSLC as the global mean barystatic sea level change, and SLC as the local barystatic sea level change, unless otherwise specified.

\subsection{Sea level physics in relation to ice sheet change}\label{sec:slp}

I shall be working under the assumptions that the timescales for modern ice-driven sea level change are short enough that the Earth's response to the ice mass change is elastic, after accounting for GIA, and that the fingerprint problem is suitably linear with small changes in sea level resulting in no appreciable change in the shoreline geometry \autocite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}.

For glacial isostatic adjustment, \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} uses \citetitle{royICE7G2018} \autocite{royICE7G2018} from \textcite{royGlacialIsostaticAdjustment2015}. The elastic assumption is broadly valid for modern ice-driven sea level change, as the timescales of interest are on the order of decades to centuries, which is short compared to the timescales of viscoelastic response of the Earth. However, it has been suggested that beneath West Antarctica this assumption could fall down due to lower viscosities \autocites{kaufmannLateralViscosityVariations2005}.

Following the assumptions and derivation detailed in \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations}, which follow \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}, SL is defined upon an equipotential surface,

\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{x}+\SL\hat{\mathbf{r}}) = \Phi_0,
\end{equation}

where $\Phi$ is the gravitational potential, $\mathbf{x}$ is the position vector, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is the unit vector in the radial direction, and $\Phi_0$ is a constant representing the potential at the sea surface. Under suitably small changes to ice thickness (see derivation), SLC is given by

\begin{equation}
  \slc = -\frac{1}{g}(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla\Phi + \phi + \psi)
  + \frac{\Phi_g}{g}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{u}$ is the solid Earth displacement, $\nabla\Phi$ is the gradient of the gravitational potential, $\phi$ is the perturbation to the gravitational potential due to the change in mass distribution, $\psi$ is the perturbation to the centrifugal potential due to polar wander, and $\Phi_g$ is the shift in the reference equipotential value, fixed by conservation of mass.

When calculating SSHC, we need to account for the fact that the measurement is relative to a reference ellipsoid rather than the solid Earth surface. Under the further approximation that $\nabla\Phi$ is radial at the Earth's surface, and following the convention of \textcite{al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023} of removing the centrifugal contribution so as to isolate the mass redistribution signal (see \autoref{sec:sea_level_equations} for details), SSHC simplifies to

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \frac{\Phi_g}{g} - \frac{\phi}{g}
\end{equation}

which is equivalent to

\begin{equation}
  \sshc = \slc + \mathbf{u} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \frac{\psi}{g}.
\end{equation}

This result is physically significant: SSHC depends only on the perturbation to the gravitational potential $\phi$, with no dependence on solid surface displacement, $\mathbf{u}$, or centrifugal potential perturbation, $\psi$. This distinction between SLC and SSHC is central to both the systematic errors investigated in this work and to building operators that measure the effects of ice sheet change on the sea surface using satellite altimetry.
