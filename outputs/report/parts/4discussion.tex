\subsection{Error of using SSHC as a proxy for SLC in GMSL estimation}

The key result from the per-source error characterisation is that the proxy error differs in both sign and magnitude across ice sources: Greenland melt leads to a systematic underestimation of GMSLC, while West and East Antarctic sources produce a smaller bias of opposite sign. This source dependence means that the SSHC proxy error cannot be corrected by a single scalar factor; the correction would itself depend on the unknown spatial distribution of ice loss. This matches the findings of \textcite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018, al-attarReciprocitySensitivityKernels2023}.

At the global scale the resulting error is modest, on the order of 1-10\% depending on expected value and uncertainty of the GMSLC; however, regional averages exhibit substantially larger and spatially variable biases, since SSHC and SLC differ by the solid-earth deformation and geoid perturbation terms, which are spatially heterogeneous functions of the ice load distribution. This motivates a more principled inference framework in which the full fingerprint physics is incorporated into the estimation procedure.


\subsection{Inversion for ice thickness change from ocean altimetry}

The recovery of the simple inversion is good, showing that the Bayesian framework is able to effectively use the ocean altimetry data to constrain the ice thickness change. The sensitivity tests are suggestive that even with poor calibration of the priors, the ocean altimetry is informative enough to constrain the ice thickness change, which is encouraging for the application of this method to real data where the priors are likely to be imperfect. The repeatability of this across multiple inversion suggests that this is not a feature of a single true data source, but rather a general feature of the method.

The improved $z$-scores from the Bayesian inversion suggest it is more effective at recovering the true GMSLC contribution from ice melt than the SSHC proxy method, as it correctly accounts for the non-uniform spatial structure of the sea level fingerprints associated with different ice sources. The SSHC proxy method's large $z$-scores indicate that it is systematically underestimating the uncertainty, likely due to its reliance on the SSHC proxy, which does not capture the full complexity of the SLC response to ice melt. The Bayesian framework, by contrast, incorporates a more comprehensive model of the SLC response, leading to more accurate uncertainty estimates and improved recovery of the true GMSLC contribution. Well-calibrated uncertainty is practically significant: overly confident GMSLC estimates propagate through to sea level budget closure and coastal hazard projections, where they can mask discrepancies and mislead risk assessments.

The approach here also addresses a limitation of \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026}, who discretise the Greenland Ice Sheet into a small number of uncorrelated mass change parameters. As \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010} discuss, finite-dimensional parameterisations can produce posteriors whose apparent tightness reflects the geometry of the discretisation rather than genuine information in the data. Working in a function space with spatially correlated priors avoids this: the posterior covariance inherits structure from the prior rather than from an arbitrary grid, and the sensitivity tests demonstrate that this structure is robust to prior misspecification.

The computational cost of the inference is sensitive to the choice of prior covariance amplitude: conjugate gradient iteration counts ranged from 47 (amplitude multiplier 0.5$\times$) to 610 (multiplier 5.0$\times$), while length-scale and mean-offset variations had comparatively little effect on convergence. This has a practical implication for the application of this method to real data: careful calibration of the prior amplitude is important for computational efficiency, even if GMSL recovery is robust across the same range of parameters.

\subsection{Joint inversion using ice, firn, and ocean dynamic changes}

The inversions show good recovery of the detail and good sensitivity when using all data types. There are clear trade-offs between the model parameters, as expected, and the method does well to disentangle these variable sources.

The poor recovery of Greenland in the joint inversion is consistent with the observation that GIS dynamics are concentrated at narrow outlet glacier margins, which require either a shorter prior length scale or a finer spherical harmonic truncation to resolve. The smooth spatial prior used here suppresses such localised signals. This is not a fundamental limitation of the framework but rather a prior specification issue, and targeted prior choices for Greenland could improve recovery.

Knock-out tests show that there is information gained from each data type, specifically ice altimetry constraining the trade-off between the different densities effectively. The supplementary figure shows that SSH altimetry is critical for constraining ocean dynamic topography (ODT), which makes intuitive sense as it provides the spatial coverage needed to separate ocean dynamic signals from ice-driven fingerprints.

The loss in ODT detail is acceptable, given the altimetry noise parameter (1\,mm standard deviation) and so only recovering features over this noise level is expected. This is also in part a consequence of jointly inverting for multiple sources, which introduces additional degrees of freedom and can lead to some loss of detail in any individual component.
