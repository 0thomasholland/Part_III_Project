\subsection{Error Quantification}

\subsubsection{Per-source proxy errors}

\autoref{fig:simple_major_source} shows the relative error of estimating GMSLC from the integral average of SSHC over the altimetry-observable ocean for each major ice sheet independently.

At the standard altimetry latitude range of $60$--$75^\circ$ \autocite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018}, the GIS contribution is underestimated by 5.8--8.2\%, the EAIS contribution is underestimated by 0--2\%, and the WAIS contribution ranges from a 2.3\% overestimation to a 0.5\% underestimation. The GIS and EAIS errors increase with the latitude of the altimetry boundary. The WAIS error changes sign near $72^\circ$, transitioning from a slight overestimation at lower latitudes to a slight underestimation at higher latitudes.

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Spatial structure of the proxy error}

To illustrate how the proxy error manifests spatially, \autoref{fig:simple_load_to_obs} shows the expected fields from a synthetic scenario in which a spatially uniform ice change, standardised to $\mathcal{N}(10\,\text{mm},\,1\,\text{mm}^2)$ GMSLC, is propagated forwards. The top row shows the ice thickness change expectation and the resulting SLC field; the middle row shows the corresponding SSHC field and the SSHC observed within the $\pm66^\circ$ altimetry band. The true GMSLC expectation is 9.951~mm with a standard deviation of 0.998~mm, while the SSHC-based estimate yields 9.831~mm with a standard deviation of 0.988~mm, confirming a small but consistent negative bias (bottom panel).

\subsubsection{Error under source uncertainty}

\autoref{fig:gaussian_uniform} extends this analysis to the case where the ice source distribution is itself uncertain, represented as a Gaussian measure rather than a fixed field. The figure shows the true and estimated GMSLC distributions, together with the error distribution, at altimetry latitude cutoffs of $66^\circ$ and $80^\circ$.

\begin{figure}[htp]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{double_distribution_66_80.pdf}
	\caption{True and estimated GMSLC distributions, with corresponding error distributions, at altimetry latitude cutoffs of $66^\circ$ (top) and $80^\circ$ (bottom), for a rotationally invariant Gaussian ice thickness prior standardised to 10~mm $\pm$ 1~mm GMSLC.}\label{fig:gaussian_uniform}
\end{figure}

At $66^\circ$, the estimated GMSLC distribution is shifted slightly below the true distribution, with the error distribution centred near zero but with a small negative bias. At $80^\circ$, the separation between the true and estimated distributions increases, and the error distribution widens and shifts further from zero, reflecting the larger proxy error incurred when the altimetry domain extends closer to the ice sheets. Over multiple runs, the general trend of a bias and increasing error with higher latitude cutoffs is consistent, confirming the systematic nature of the proxy error under source uncertainty.

\subsection{Sensitivity tests}

\notebookcite{thomasholland07InversionSensitivityipynb2026}

Inversions in this section are run at a spherical harmonic truncation of 128 for computational speed. This is not expected to affect the sensitivity test results, which concern relative changes as parameters vary rather than absolute values.

\autoref{fig:sensitivity-z_score} shows the posterior z-scores across all sensitivity cases. In every case the posterior contains the true GMSL value and shows no systematic bias, demonstrating that the Bayesian inversion is robust to prior misspecification. The accurate prior case best recovers the true field, as expected. Spatial recovery across all cases is shown in the supplementary figures (\autoref{fig:sensitivity-true-inversion} to \autoref{fig:sensitivity-variance}).

The three parameter groups differ in character: length scale affects spatial structure but has little effect on GMSL recovery; mean offset tests a worst-case scenario where the true value lies outside the prior support; and covariance amplitude has the most practical implications, with higher variance cases being poorly conditioned and requiring substantially more conjugate gradient iterations to converge (47, 197, and 610 iterations for multipliers of 0.5, 2.0, and 5.0 respectively). The prior mean offset and length scale have comparatively little effect on convergence behaviour.

\subsection{Joint inversion using ice, firn, and ocean dynamic changes}

\autoref{fig:joint-inversion} shows the results of a joint inversion for ice thickness change, firn thickness change, and ocean dynamic changes from a synthetic test using ocean altimetry, ice altimetry, and GRACE data. The posterior expectation captures the spatial structure of ice and firn thickness change well. However, some regions such as Greenland show poor recovery, likely because the smooth spatial prior poorly resolves the localised margin thinning characteristic of GIS dynamics.
