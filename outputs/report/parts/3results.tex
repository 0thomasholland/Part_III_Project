\subsection{Error Quantification}

\subsubsection{Per-source proxy errors}

\autoref{fig:simple_major_source} shows the relative error of estimating GMSL from the integral average of SSHC over the altimetry-observable ocean for each major ice sheet independently.

At the standard altimetry latitude range of $60$--$75^\circ$ \autocite{lickleyBiasEstimatesGlobal2018}, the GIS contribution is underestimated by 5.8--8.2\%, the EAIS contribution is underestimated by 0--2\%, and the WAIS contribution ranges from a 2.3\% overestimation to a 0.5\% underestimation. The GIS and EAIS errors increases with the latitude of the altimetry boundary. The WAIS error changes sign near $72^\circ$, transitioning from a slight overestimation at lower latitudes to a slight underestimation at higher latitudes.

To illustrate how the proxy error manifests spatially, \autoref{fig:simple_load_to_obs} shows the expected fields from a single synthetic scenario in which a spatially uniform ice change standardised to $\mathcal{N}(10\text{mm}, 1\text{mm}^2)$ GMSL change is propagated forwards. The resulting true GMSL expectation is 9.951~mm with a standard deviation of 0.998~mm. The SSHC-based estimate yields an expectation of 9.831~mm with a standard deviation of 0.988~mm. The error distribution is centred just below zero, confirming a small but consistent negative bias, shown in \autoref{fig:simple_error_dist}. \autoref{fig:sample_simple_load} shows a sample drawn from this distribution, which captures the spatial variability underlying these distributions.


\subsubsection{Error under source uncertainty}

\autoref{fig:gaussian_uniform} extends the per-source analysis by showing the GMSL estimation error when the ice source distribution is represented as a Gaussian measure rather than a fixed field. The figure shows the true and estimated GMSL distributions, together with the error distribution, at altimetry latitude cutoffs of $66^\circ$ and $80^\circ$.



At $66^\circ$, the estimated GMSL distribution is shifted slightly below the true distribution, with the error distribution centred near zero but with a small negative bias. At $80^\circ$, the separation between the true and estimated distributions increases, and the error distribution widens and shifts further from zero, reflecting the larger proxy error incurred when the altimetry domain extends closer to the ice sheets.


\begin{figure}[hbp]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{double_distribution_66_80.pdf}
	\caption{True and estimated GMSL distributions, with corresponding error distributions, at altimetry latitude cutoffs of $66^\circ$ (top) and $80^\circ$ (bottom), for a rotationally invariant Gaussian ice thickness prior standardised to 10~mm $\pm$ 1~mm GMSL change.}\label{fig:gaussian_uniform}
\end{figure}


Over multiple runs, we found qualitatively, this pattern varies as the GMSL estimation error varies with the underlying ice source distribution parameters. However, the general trend of a bias and increasing error with higher latitude cutoffs is consistent across runs, confirming the systematic nature of the proxy error under source uncertainty.

This section is run at a spherical harmonic truncation of 128 for computational speed, but this is not expected to affect the results of the sensitivity tests, which are focused on the relative changes in the inversion results as the parameters are varied, rather than the absolute values of the inversion results.

\autoref{fig:sensitivity-z_score} shows the posterior z-scores across all sensitivity cases. In every case the posterior contains the true GMSL value and shows no systematic bias, demonstrating that the Bayesian inversion is robust to prior mis-specification. The accurate prior case recovers the true field most tightly, as expected. Spatial recovery across all cases is shown in the supplementary figures (\autoref{fig:sensitivity-true-inversion} to \autoref{fig:sensitivity-variance}).

The three parameter groups differ in character: length scale affects spatial structure but has little effect on GMSL recovery; mean offset tests a worst-case scenario where the true value lies outside the prior support; and covariance amplitude has the most practical implications, with higher variance cases being poorly conditioned and requiring substantially more conjugate gradient iterations to converge (47, 197, and 610 iterations for multipliers of 0.5, 2.0, and 5.0 respectively). The prior mean offset and length scale have comparatively little effect on convergence behaviour.

\autoref{fig:joint-inversion} shows the results of a joint inversion for ice thickness change, firn compaction, and ocean dynamic changes from a synthetic test using ocean altimetery, tide gauges, ice altimetry, and GRACE data. The posterior expectation captures the spatial variances in ice and firn thickness change well, with some regions showing a trade off between them (e.g. Eastern most Antartica where the posterior expectation of ice thickness gain is overestimated and the firn loss is overestimated).

The ocean dynamic change is less well recovered, with the posterior expectation showing a smoother spatial structure than the true field, but still capturing the main large amplitude features of the true field.
