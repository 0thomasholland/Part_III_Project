\subsubsection{Per-source proxy errors}

\autoref{fig:simple_major_source} shows the relative error of estimating GMSL from the area-weighted average of SSHC over the altimetry-observable ocean, for uniform melt scenarios normalised to 1~mm of true GMSL change from each major ice sheet independently.

At the standard altimetry latitude range of $60$--$75^\circ$, the GrIS contribution is underestimated by 5--8\%, the EAIS contribution is underestimated by 0--2\%, and the WAIS contribution ranges from a 2.2\% overestimation to a 0.4\% underestimation depending on the latitude cutoff. The GrIS error increases monotonically with the latitude of the altimetry boundary, while the EAIS error remains comparatively flat across the tested range. The WAIS error changes sign near $40^\circ$, transitioning from a slight overestimation at lower latitudes to a slight underestimation at higher latitudes.

\subsubsection{Spatial structure of the proxy error}

To illustrate how the proxy error manifests spatially, \autoref{fig:simple_load_to_obs} shows the output fields from a single synthetic scenario in which a rotationally invariant ice thickness change is drawn from a Gaussian measure standardised to $10, \sigma = 1$~mm GMSL change. The ice load field, its corresponding SLC and SSHC fields, and the observed SSHC within the $\pm66^\circ$ altimetry band are shown.

The resulting true GMSL is 9.951~mm with a standard deviation of 0.998~mm. The SSHC-based estimate yields 9.831~mm with a standard deviation of 0.988~mm, corresponding to a percentage error of approximately 1.34\%. The error distribution is centred just below zero, confirming a small but consistent negative bias.

The spatial distribution of the error expectation field (SLC $-$ SSHC) shows a strongly negative signal at high southern latitudes proximal to Antarctica, reaching approximately $-15$~mm, with a weaker negative signal near Greenland. The remainder of the open ocean is close to zero. The pointwise standard deviation of the error field follows a qualitatively similar spatial pattern, ranging from approximately 0.75~mm across the open ocean to 2.75~mm near the Antarctic margins.

\subsubsection{Error under source uncertainty}

\autoref{fig:gaussian_uniform} extends the per-source analysis by showing the GMSL estimation error when the ice source distribution is represented as a Gaussian measure rather than a fixed field. The figure shows the true and estimated GMSL distributions, together with the error distribution, at altimetry latitude cutoffs of $66^\circ$ and $80^\circ$.

At $66^\circ$, the estimated GMSL distribution is shifted slightly below the true distribution, with the error distribution centred near zero but with a small negative bias. At $80^\circ$, the separation between the true and estimated distributions increases, and the error distribution widens and shifts further from zero, reflecting the larger proxy error incurred when the altimetry domain extends closer to the ice sheets.

\begin{figure}[!h]
	\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/double_distribution_66_80}
	\caption{True and estimated GMSL distributions, with corresponding error distributions, at altimetry latitude cutoffs of $66^\circ$ (top) and $80^\circ$ (bottom), for a rotationally invariant Gaussian ice thickness prior standardised to 10~mm $\pm$ 1~mm GMSL change.}\label{fig:gaussian_uniform}
\end{figure}

Over multiple runs, this pattern varies as the GMSL estimation error varies with the underlying ice source distribution parameters. However, the general trend of a bias and increasing error with higher latitude cutoffs is consistent across runs, confirming the systematic nature of the proxy error under source uncertainty.

\subsection{Method comparison}

% A single representative inversion example, using figure 2 - note the posterior is well-centred on the true value, the altimetry estimate is biased low, both are tight distributions

With a prior ice thickness distribution with spatial thickness variability and standardised to a GMSL contribution of 0 mm with standard deviation of 10 mm and ocean altimetry sampling of 5.0 degrees, the following results were calculated. The true data was synthetically drawn from the prior distribution, this being consistent with a well calibrated Bayesian inversion prior.

\autoref{fig:gmsl_contribution_distributions} shows the posterior distribution of ice-driven GMSL from an inversion, with the old method's estimate is also given. The Bayesian inversion's distribution contains the true value (vertical dashed line) and is much narrower than the old method's distribution, which is biased low and does not contain the true value.

\autoref{fig:z_score_method_comparison} shows the z-score distribution of the true value under the predicted distribution for both methods across 261 runs. The Bayesian inversion's z-scores cluster tightly around zero, indicating that it is unbiased and has well-calibrated uncertainty quantification. In contrast, the old method's z-scores show a broader distribution with a horizontal elongation, suggesting that it has larger variance and may be systematically biased.

The z-score statistics for the new method show a mean of 0.07 and a standard deviation of 1.21, while the old method has a mean of 0.23 and a standard deviation of 4.20. The new method's mean z-score near zero indicates that it is unbiased, with estimates centred on the true value on average. The standard deviation of z-scores near one suggests that the new method's uncertainty quantification is well-calibrated, meaning it is neither overconfident nor underconfident. In contrast, the old method's higher mean and much larger standard deviation indicate that it is biased and has poorly calibrated uncertainty quantification, often being overconfident in its estimates.

The old method is only within 1 sigma of the true value in 14.6\% of runs, while the new method is within 1 sigma in 60.5\% of runs.
