The methods developed and discussed in \autoref{sec:methods} rely upon a rigerously defined mathematical framework, which is implemented in the \citetitle{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025} python package, as described in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}. This framework allows for the consistent representation of continuous fields over the sphere, the application of linear operators to these fields, and the use of Gaussian measures to quantify uncertainty. The mathematical properties of this framework are critical to the development and implementation of the methods described in this report, and so a brief overview is given here to provide context for the subsequent sections.

\subsection{Mathematical framework}\label{sec:maths_background}

For sea level physics problems at the length and timescales involved with our problem, it is physically approriate to generate our problem on spaces where the realised feildes are guaranteed to be spatially smooth, and where the operators that we use to represent the physics of the problem are linear. This allows us to use a mathematical framework that is based on Hilbert spaces, linear operators, and Gaussian measures, which is implemented in the \citetitle{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025} python package which \citetitle{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} is built upon. For a full explanation of this motivation see section 2.3.2 of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}.

To represent this type of continuous field over the sphere (taken to be a unit sphere, $\mathbb{S}^2$, scaled to the dimensions of the Earth) in a mathematically rigorous way, we work within a Sobolev space, $h \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$, where the index $s$ controls the smoothness of admissible fields.

This framework accommodates both deterministic fields, which enter as fixed elements of $H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$, and  Gaussian measures, which we use to quantify our understandning of various parameters as described later in \autoref{sec:bay}. These Gaussian measures are on $H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$, which are characterised by its mean $m \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$ and covariance operator $\mathcal{C}$:



\begin{equation}
\mu = \mathcal{N}(m, \mathcal{C})
\end{equation}

There are suitable conditions that $\mathcal{C}$ must satisfy to be a valid covariance operator on an infinite-dimensional space, however this is handeled by \citetitle{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025} and so is not discussed here. However, for the case where custom covariance structures are used, it is important to ensure that these conditions are satisfied to ensure that the resulting measure is well defined. This is a critical factor worth emphasising: linear operators that act on Gaussian measures ensure that the resulting pushforward measure is still a valid Gaussian measure, which is guaranteed by the properties of linear operators and the conditions on covariance operators.

This property of linear operators being able to act on both scalar and gaussian fields allows uncertainty to be propagated consistently through the forward model and for linear operators to apply to both, regardless of whether the input is deterministic or stochastic. For example, a known ice thickness distribution enters as a fixed field, while our prior belief about ice thickness change is represented as a Gaussian measure over the same space, but the same linear operators can be applied to both without modification. This ensures a consistent mathematical treatment of all components of the model.

Linear operators map between fields, with their adjoints mapping the reverse way, allowing for the consistent propagation of uncertainty and the application of the forward model in both directions. For example, the forward operator that maps ice thickness change to sea level change has an adjoint that maps from the data space back to the model space, which is essential for calculating the Kalman gain and performing Bayesian updates.

\begin{align}
    A: X &\rightarrow Y \\
    A^*: Y &\rightarrow X \\
    \braket{Ax, y}_Y &= \braket{x, A^*y}_X
\end{align}


The action of a linear operator on a Gaussian measure is well-defined: if $\mu_X \in X$, then the pushforward under $A$ is:

\begin{align}
	\mu_X &= \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \mathcal{C}) \in X \\
	A \mu &= \mathcal{N}(A\mathbf{m}, A\mathcal{C}A^*) \in Y
\end{align}

This gives the posterior mean value of $A\mathbf{m}$ and posterior covariance of $A\mathcal{C}A^*$, and as these are both well defined, this means that the resulting measure is a valid Gaussian measure on $Y$. This is a critical property that underpins the entire Bayesian inversion framework, as it allows us to propagate uncertainty through the forward model and to perform updates in a mathematically consistent way.

It is often the case where we wish to extract scalar-valued quantities of interest, such as the Global Mean Sea Level (GMSL). These can be represented as continuous linear functionals $\phi: H^s(\mathbb{S}^2) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. By the Riesz representation theorem, any such functional can be written as:

\begin{equation}
\phi(h) = \braket{h, k}_{H^s}
\end{equation}
for a unique representer $k \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$. A representer for GMSL, for example, would be a constant function over the ocean area and zero elsewhere, such that the inner product with a sea level change field yields the spatial average over the oceans. This allows us to compute the distribution of GMSL change directly from the distribution of sea level change.

For most readers, the mathematical framing given should be sufficient to understand the remainder of the paper, however for full details for the mathematical framework surrounding the underlying python packages can be found in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026}, which lies outside the scope of this report. Other useful texts for a wider background are \citetitle{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010} \autocite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010} for Bayseain inverion on infintie dimensions, \citetitle{parkerGeophysicalInverseTheory1994} \autocite{parkerGeophysicalInverseTheory1994} for geophysical inverse theory, and \citetitle{rudinFunctionalAnalysis1991} \autocite{rudinFunctionalAnalysis1991} for background on funcational anaysis and introduction to Hilbert Spaces, linear operators, Sobolev spaces, Adjoints, etc.

Calculating ice sheet change from the change in ocean altimetry data is an inverse problem, which can be framed using Bayesian inference.

A Bayesian view of probability differs from a frequentists' perspective. Frequentist statistics interprets probability as long-run frequency, treating parameters as fixed and data as random, using $p$-values and confidence intervals to accept or reject hypotheses. Conversely, Bayesian statistics treats parameters as random, incorporating prior beliefs and updating them with data to produce a posterior distribution, allowing for direct probability statements about hypotheses. This has the benefit that the posterior distributions give quantifications about the uncertainties of the view of world produced by the method, and that, given suitable uncertainties are quantified within our prior, we can directly understand the uncertainties of our results.

Bayesian inference is framed as using a specific implementation of Bayes' theorem:

\begin{equation}
	p(\text{model} | \text{data}) = \dfrac{p(\text{data} | \text{model})p(\text{model})}{p(\text{data})}
\end{equation}

Where the data is the observed data that we collect, or synthetically generate when developing the methods, and the model is the parameters we define across the model space.

Bayesian inversion is the specific application of this framework to inverse problems, where, rather than seeking a single best-fit solution, it characterises the entire probability distribution of unknown physical parameters from noisy data. To summarise in a simple case, if we state our prior knowledge of a model space in terms of a Gaussian measure given as:

\begin{equation*}
	m \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}, \mathcal{C}_m)
\end{equation*}

Which is suitably pushed forward through our physical system and measured in the data space using a combined forward operator $A$, with some random Gaussian noise associated with the measurements, given as a vector, $n$, with expectation $\bar{n}$ and covariance $\mathcal{C}_n$. This would result in the data space vector, $d$, being:

\begin{align*}
	d &= Am +z\\
	\to  d &\sim \mathcal{N}(A\bar{m} + \bar{n},\quad A\mathcal{C}_mA^* + \mathcal{C}_n)
\end{align*}

Using the form of Bayes' theorem implemented in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} originally presented in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}, the posterior measure of our updated view of the model space given an observation, $d_{obs}$, of the data space would be:

\begin{align}
	m_p \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}+K(d_{obs}-A\bar{m}-\bar{n},\quad (1-KA)\mathcal{C}_{m}))
\end{align}

Where $K$ is the Kalman gain, which is the information gained by the observation, is given as:

\begin{equation}
	K = \mathcal{C}_{m}A^*(A\mathcal{C}_{m}A^*+\mathcal{C}_{n})^{-1}
\end{equation}

The priors that are defined for the model space are critical to the results of the inversion, as they encode our prior beliefs about the system and can significantly influence the posterior distribution.

In this context, informative priors are those that incorporate specific priori knowledge about the physical system, such as known spatial patterns of ice thickness change or constraints based on physical laws. These priors can help to regularise the inversion and guide it towards physically plausible solutions, especially in cases where the data is sparse or noisy.

Using informative priors, however, is not without its flaws, and as discussed in \textcite{vandeschootBayesianStatisticsModelling2021}, it is important to assess the impact of how informative priors affect the posterior distribution, as this is a key way in which overly biased results can be introduced. The disctinction is nuanced but for our purposes can be summarised as restrictions based on what we think happen in a physical system are valid, but restrictions based on what we think has happened to generate our data are not.  For example, when generating ice sheet priors, it is physically motivated to restrict ice loss to ice sheet margins (see \autoref{sec:ice_thickness_priors}), however biasing these priors to have a expectation of ice loss is not, as this is a restriction based on what we think has happened to generate our data, and thus can lead to overly biased results.

There is also a subtle distinction to be made between restricting priors based on knowledge of the physical system and behaviours, as just described, and restricting the priors, such as using artificial discretization schemes. The former is defensible within a Baysean framework, however the latter is a restriction imposed by computational or mathematical restriction, and, as discussed in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}, can result in artefacts or overly constrained posteriors due to these restriction.

This where an important distinction between this method and recently released method by \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} have used Bayesian inversion, as they have used a discrete scheme for inverting ice thickness change over Greenland, the method outlined in this report in arguably an improvement of their method, as results in a more physically motivated prior, and thus a more physically motivated posterior, as well as avoiding the potential artefacts that can arise from discretization schemes. However, it is important to note that the method outlined in this report is not without its own limitations and assumptions, and further work is needed to fully understand the implications of these choices on the results of the inversion.
