\subsection{Mathematical framework}\label{sec:maths_background}

For sea-level physics at the relevant lengthscales and timescales, it is physically appropriate to pose our problem on spaces where the realised fields are guaranteed to be spatially smooth, and where linear operators represent the physical mapping between these spaces.

To represent a continuous field over the sphere ($\mathbb{S}^2$ scaled to the dimensions of the Earth) we work within a Sobolev space, $h \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$, where the index $s$ controls the smoothness of admissible fields.

Linear operators (e.g. $A$) map between these spaces, with their adjoints mapping back to the original space (e.g. $A^*$). For example, if we have a linear operator $A: X \to Y$ that maps from a space $X$ to a space $Y$, then its adjoint $A^*: Y \to X$ maps back from $Y$ to $X$.

This framework accommodates both deterministic fields, which enter as fixed elements of $H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$, and Gaussian measures, which are used to quantify our understanding of various parameters, as described later in \autoref{sec:bay}. These Gaussian measures are characterised by their mean, $m \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$, and covariance operator $\mathcal{C}$, and are written as

\begin{equation}
\mu = \mathcal{N}(m, \mathcal{C}).
\end{equation}

There are suitable conditions that $\mathcal{C}$ must satisfy to be a valid covariance operator on an infinite-dimensional space, which are ensured by \citetitle{al-attarPygeoinfPythonLibrary2025}. An example is the heat kernel covariance structure. Let $\Delta$ be the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the sphere, $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ parameters controlling the pointwise standard deviation and the lengthscale of spatial correlations respectively, and $\braket{\cdot,\cdot}$ the inner product of the space (a generalisation of the dot product \autocite{parkerGeophysicalInverseTheory1994}). The heat kernel covariance then takes the form

\begin{equation}
  \covariance{} = \frac{\alpha^2}{\braket{e^{-\lambda^2\Delta}\delta_x,\delta_x}} e^{-\lambda^2 \Delta}\label{eq:heat_kernel_covariance}
\end{equation} This covariance structure ensures the resulting Gaussian measure is well-defined and rotationally invariant (covariance equal at any point). It is useful for geophysical problems, encoding the physical intuition of scaled point-wise variance and spatial correlation that decays with distance.

Linear operators acting on Gaussian measures produce a valid Gaussian pushforward measure on the target space:

\begin{align}
\mu_X &= \mathcal{N}(m, \mathcal{C}) \in X, \\
A\mu_X &= \mathcal{N}(Am, A\mathcal{C}A^*) \in Y.
\end{align}


That is, pushing $\mu_X$ forward through $A$ transforms the mean and covariance consistently, and the result remains a well-defined Gaussian measure in $Y$. This closure under linear maps holds uniformly, whether the input is a deterministic field or a Gaussian measure: the same operator formulation applies to both without distinction. For example, a known ice thickness distribution and a prior distribution over ice thickness change are acted upon by the same forward operators, giving the framework its mathematical consistency. As shown in \autoref{sec:bay}, it is precisely this property that permits the posterior distribution to be computed in closed form.


It is often the case that we wish to extract scalar-valued quantities of interest, such as the Global Mean Sea Level (GMSL). These can be represented as continuous linear functionals $\ell: H^s(\mathbb{S}^2) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. By the Riesz representation theorem \autocite{rudinFunctionalAnalysis1991}, any such functional can be written as

\begin{equation}
\ell(h) = \braket{h, k}_{H^s}
\end{equation}

for a unique representer $k \in H^s(\mathbb{S}^2)$. A representer for GMSL, for example, would be a constant function over the ocean area and zero elsewhere, such that the inner product with a sea level change field yields the spatial average over the oceans. This allows us to compute the distribution of GMSL change directly from the distribution of sea level change.

Recovering ice sheet change from altimetry is an inverse problem, which can be framed using Bayesian inference.

Bayesian inference is framed as a specific implementation of Bayes' theorem:

\begin{equation}
	p(\text{model} | \text{data}) = \dfrac{p(\text{data} | \text{model})p(\text{model})}{p(\text{data})}
\end{equation}

where the data are the observations we collect, or synthetically generate when developing the methods, and the model comprises the parameters we define across the model space, and $p(\cdot)$ is the probability of the argument. The likelihood, $p(\text{data} | \text{model})$, is the probability of observing the data given a particular model, and the prior, $p(\text{model})$, is our initial belief about the model before seeing the data. The posterior, $p(\text{model} | \text{data})$, is our updated belief about the model after observing the data.

Bayesian inversion is the specific application of this framework to inverse problems, where, rather than seeking a single best-fit solution, it characterises the entire probability distribution of unknown physical parameters from noisy data. To show this simply, if we state our prior knowledge of a model space in terms of a Gaussian measure, with some expectation $\bar{m}$ and covariance $\mathcal{C}_m$, the model space can be represented as

\begin{equation*}
	m \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}, \mathcal{C}_m)
\end{equation*}

which we push forward using an operator $A$ that represents the physical relation between model and data spaces. Some random noise is associated with the measurements, given as a vector, $n$, and can sensibly be assumed Gaussian. With an expectation $\bar{n}$ and covariance $\mathcal{C}_n$ of the noise, the data space vector, $d$, would be

\begin{align*}
	d &= Am +n,\\
	\implies  d &\sim \mathcal{N}(A\bar{m} + \bar{n},\quad A\mathcal{C}_mA^* + \mathcal{C}_n).
\end{align*}

Using a form of Bayes' theorem implemented in \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} originally presented in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010} for the inverse problem, we calculate the posterior distribution of the model space in closed form as a Gaussian measure with mean and covariance given as

\begin{align}
	m_p \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar{m}+K(d_{obs}-A\bar{m}-\bar{n}),\quad (1-KA)\mathcal{C}_{m}))
\end{align}

where $K$ is the Kalman gain, defined as

\begin{equation}
	K = \mathcal{C}_{m}A^*(A\mathcal{C}_{m}A^*+\mathcal{C}_{n})^{-1}.
\end{equation}

Priors defined in the model space are critical to the inversion results, as they encode our beliefs about the system and significantly influence the posterior. The choice of prior is therefore discussed in detail in \autoref{sec:ice_thickness_priors}.

Priors that incorporate specific prior knowledge about the physical system are known as informative priors. An example is using spatial patterns of ice thickness change or constraints based on physical laws to restrict prior distributions. These informative priors can help to regularise the inversion and guide it towards physically plausible solutions, especially in cases where the data is sparse or very noisy.

Using informative priors, however, is not without its flaws. It is important to assess the impact of how informative priors affect the posterior distribution, as this is an unintended way in which overly biased results can be introduced \autocite{vandeschootBayesianStatisticsModelling2021}. The distinction is nuanced, but for our purposes can be summarised as restrictions based on what we think happens in a physical system are valid, but restrictions based on what we think has happened to generate our data are not. For example, when generating ice sheet priors, it is physically motivated to restrict ice loss to ice sheet margins, however biasing these priors to have a expectation of ice loss, due to some understanding of our data, is not, as this is a restriction based on what we think has happened to generate our data, and thus can lead to overly biased results.

A further distinction separates restricting priors based on physical knowledge, as just described, from restricting them through artificial discretisation schemes. The former is defensible within a Bayesian framework; the latter is a restriction imposed by computational or mathematical need, and, as discussed in \textcite{stuartInverseProblemsBayesian2010}, can result in artefacts or overly constrained posteriors.
