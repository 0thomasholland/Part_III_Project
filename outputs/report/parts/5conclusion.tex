The central limitation motivating this work is that existing approaches to sea level attribution either neglect the full physics of gravitationally self-consistent sea level change, or impose prior constraints on ice geometry as hard, external judgements rather than probabilistic beliefs. The SSH correction proxy exemplifies the first problem, and the discrete parameterisation approach of \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} illustrates the second: both produce usable results under favourable conditions, but neither provides a principled account of uncertainty that propagates through the physics of the forward problem.

The results presented here address both limitations in turn. The error analysis shows that the SSHC proxy introduces source-dependent biases in GMSLC calculations toward underestimation of approximately 5.8--8.2\% for GIS melt or 0.5--2\% for EAIS and over estimations of approximately 1--2\% for WAIS. This source-dependence itself precludes any scalar correction; the bias depends on where the ice is melting, and correcting for it requires knowledge of the very quantity being estimated. The Bayesian inversion result shows that replacing the proxy with a full inversion substantially improves posterior calibration: 60.5\% of synthetic twin experiments fall within one posterior standard deviation compared to 14.6\% under the proxy method (n=261), a result that constitutes direct empirical evidence that the framework produces well-calibrated uncertainties rather than merely plausible ones. The joint inversion over ice thickness change, firn compaction, and ocean dynamic topography extends this further: knock-out tests demonstrate that each data type contributes non-redundant information, and the posterior successfully disentangles contributions that are entangled at the level of the observations. This is the qualitative advance over both the proxy approach and the finite-dimensional parameterisation approach, which treats ocean dynamic corrections as a preprocessing step and thereby severs the statistical coupling between signals that a joint inference should exploit. The sensitivity tests indicate that the method is robust to prior mis-specification, though the computational cost of the inference is sensitive to the choice of prior covariance amplitude, which suggests that careful calibration of this aspect of the prior is important for computational reasons but not scientific.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the infinite-dimensional Bayesian framework of \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} provides three things that neither the SSHC proxy nor the discrete parameterisation approach can offer simultaneously: principled uncertainty propagation through the full forward physics, spatially correlated posteriors whose structure is inherited from the prior rather than imposed by a discretisation grid, and joint inference over competing signals with formal uncertainty quantification on the trade-offs between them. The Richards curve prior developed here adds a further element: rather than encoding physical knowledge as a hard geometric constraint, it encodes it as a spatially varying belief about the plausibility of mass change, conditioned on ice thickness. This is a more honest representation of what is actually known about ice sheet dynamics.

The natural complement to these synthetic results is the proof-of-concept inversion against real altimetry data performed by \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026}, which demonstrates that the SSH fingerprint of GIS melt is recoverable from satellite observations when appropriate corrections are applied. That work establishes the observational basis; the framework developed here provides the inferential machinery. Combining the two would give a substantially more reliable estimate of GIS mass flux than either can produce in isolation: the physical realism of the forward model and the calibrated uncertainty quantification developed here would address the known limitations of the discrete parameterisation approach, while the real-data corrections and validation of \textcite{coulsonInvertingSeaSurface2026} would replace the synthetic twin experiments with observationally grounded inference. A preliminary test against the \textcite{copernicusclimatechangeserviceGLOBALOCEANGRIDDED2021} dataset is encouraging and consistent with independent estimates, though a full application requires the data corrections and systematic validation that lie beyond the scope of this report.

Several directions for future work follow naturally from the results. The sensitivity of the joint inversion to prior choices, particularly the spatial correlation structure and amplitude of the firn and ocean dynamic fields, requires a dedicated sensitivity study; the knock-out tests suggest these choices matter for source separation even where the total sea level budget is well-constrained. More broadly, the composite model space formulation is not limited to the three components considered here: the thermosteric and halosteric contributions to ocean dynamic topography identified by \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} could be incorporated as additional model subspaces, offering a route toward a more complete accounting of the sea level budget within the same inferential framework. The tools developed within this report and within \textcite{heathcoteScaleableBayesianFramework2026} and \textcite{al-attarPySLFPPythonSea2025} provide the foundation for that future work.
